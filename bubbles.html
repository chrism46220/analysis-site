Revolutionary Technologies & Financial Bubbles

An examination of whether the current AI revolution is likely to create a bubble — viewed through 700 years of history

Throughout history, transformative technologies have reshaped economies and societies. A recurring pattern accompanies many of these revolutions: a wave of speculative investment that inflates asset prices to unsustainable levels before collapsing — a financial bubble.

This report surveys major revolutionary technologies from the 1300s onward, identifies which generated bubbles, and uses the historical pattern to assess whether the current AI revolution is likely to produce one.

A note on definitions: a “bubble” here means a sustained, dramatic rise in asset prices (stocks, commodities, or land) driven by speculative enthusiasm rather than fundamental value, followed by a sharp decline. Not every technology triggered a bubble; some developed more gradually or through private capital without stock-market mania.

1. The Technologies and Their Bubble Status

Printing Press c. 1440

Technology: Movable-type printing press invented by Johannes Gutenberg.

NO BUBBLE

The printing press spread over centuries, not years. Printing was a craft business, not a joint-stock company with publicly traded shares. Capital requirements were modest, and no speculative mania formed around it. Its economic impact was profound but gradual.

Maritime Exploration & Overseas Trade 1500s–1600s

Technology: Long-distance ocean navigation, improved shipbuilding, colonial trade routes.

BUBBLE — South Sea Bubble (1720)

The South Sea Company (chartered 1711) was granted a monopoly to trade with Spanish America. Its stock rose from £128 in January 1720 to over £1,000 in August before collapsing to under £150 by December. While the underlying technology (maritime exploration/trade) was real, the speculation vastly exceeded any plausible profits. Dozens of fraudulent “bubble companies” sprang up — including “a company for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is.”

Tulip Cultivation 1630s

Technology: Importation and cultivation of exotic tulip bulbs from the Ottoman Empire.

BUBBLE — Tulip Mania (1636–1637)

This is the most famous early speculative mania. Tulip bulbs, particularly rare varieties, traded for extraordinary sums — a single Semper Augustus bulb sold for 5,500 guilders (roughly the price of a house). The market collapsed in February 1637. Note: this was a commodities speculation rather than a technology-driven stock bubble, but it established the psychological template for all later manias.

Steam Engine 1712 onward, widespread by 1770s–1800s

Technology: Newcomen (1712) and Watt (1765) steam engines, powering factories, mines, and transport.

NO DIRECT BUBBLE

The steam engine developed over ~100 years. Its deployment was financed by industrialists, existing partnerships, and canal/railway companies rather than speculative public stock offerings in “steam engine companies.” However, steam enabled subsequent transport bubbles.

Canals 1760s–1790s

Technology: Artificial waterways for bulk freight transport, enabled by civil engineering advances.

BUBBLE — Canal Mania (1792–1793, and 1820s)

Britain’s canal network expanded rapidly. Speculative canal company shares soared in the early 1790s and again in the 1820s. Many projected canals were never built; investors lost heavily. Canal Mania established the pattern of infrastructure speculation that would recur with railways.

Railways 1825 onward

Technology: Steam locomotives on iron rails for high-volume land transport.

BUBBLE — Railway Mania (1844–1846)

The most spectacular infrastructure bubble in British history. Between 1844 and 1846, Parliament authorized over 8,000 miles of new railway — much of it never built. Railway shares dominated the London Stock Exchange. The market peaked in summer 1845, collapsed by autumn, and wiped out a generation of small investors. Railway Mania is the canonical example of technology-meets-infrastructure speculation. The railway bubble also appeared in the United States, where railroad stocks drove multiple boom-bust cycles in the 1850s, 1870s, and 1880s.

Telegraph 1837 onward

Technology: Electrical transmission of messages over wires (Morse, Cooke & Wheatstone).

MINOR BUBBLE

Telegraph companies did attract speculative capital — the Electric Telegraph Company (UK) and various US telegraph companies saw stock-price surges. However, the telegraph was quickly recognized as genuinely useful and profitable; the speculation was moderate compared to railways or the internet. By the 1850s, there were too many telegraph companies, leading to consolidation rather than a dramatic crash.

Electricity 1880s–1890s

Technology: Electric lighting, motors, and power generation and distribution.

MILD BUBBLE

The “War of the Currents” and rapid electrification created enormous excitement. Edison Electric Light (later General Electric) saw major stock appreciation. The 1890s included a speculative boom in electricity stocks, particularly in 1892–1893. Many early electric utilities failed, but the sector’s genuine growth limited the crash.

Bicycle 1890s

Technology: Safety bicycle (chain-driven, equal wheels), pneumatic tires.

BUBBLE — Bicycle Bubble (1896)

The bicycle craze of the 1890s created an enormous stock bubble, particularly in Britain. The UK’s Pneumatic Tyre Company (Dunlop) saw its stock rise from £3 to £14 before crashing. The bubble burst in 1896–1897, with many manufacturers going bankrupt. This is one of the clearest technology-stock bubbles in history.

Automobile 1900s–1920s

Technology: Internal combustion engine, mass production (Ford Model T), personal transportation.

PARTIAL — rode 1920s boom

Automobile stocks were among the hottest in the 1920s bull market. Companies like Dodge, Chrysler, and General Motors saw enormous gains. However, the auto industry genuinely transformed the economy, and many companies survived. The crash of 1929 took auto stocks down along with everything else, but there was no singular “Auto Mania” — the bubble was broader.

Radio 1920s

Technology: Wireless broadcasting of audio signals.

BUBBLE — Radio Stock Mania (1928–1929)

Radio was the internet of the 1920s. RCA (Radio Corporation of America) was the defining stock of the era, rising from $11 in 1921 to $114 in 1928 and then to $505 in 1929 before crashing to $10 in 1932. Radio manufacturing companies proliferated. The enthusiasm was immense and the collapse devastating. This is a textbook technology bubble.

Aviation 1920s–1930s

Technology: Powered heavier-than-air flight for commercial and military use.

BUBBLE — Lindbergh Boom (1928–1929)

Charles Lindbergh’s solo transatlantic flight in 1927 ignited a frenzy in aviation stocks. The boom was intense but short-lived — most aviation stocks collapsed in the 1929 crash and the Depression that followed, with many airlines going bankrupt.

Computers 1950s–1960s

Technology: Electronic digital computers for data processing.

MILD — Electronics Boom (1959–1962)

The 1959–1962 period saw a speculative frenzy in “electronics” stocks on Wall Street. Companies added “tronics” to their names to attract investors. IBM was the dominant performer but was fundamentally sound. The bubble was smaller and more contained than later tech manias. Some stocks crashed in 1962, but IBM and others continued to grow.

Nifty Fifty Growth Stocks 1970–1973

Technology: Broad set of growth technologies including Polaroid (instant photography), Xerox (photocopying), IBM (computers).

BUBBLE — Nifty Fifty (1970–1973)

The “Nifty Fifty” were ~50 high-growth stocks that investors treated as “one-decision” buy-and-hold stocks. They traded at extreme multiples — Polaroid at 90x earnings, Xerox at 50x. When the 1973–74 bear market hit, they fell 50–80%. This was a classic growth-stock bubble anchored in real technology companies.

Biotechnology 1980s–1990s

Technology: Genetic engineering, recombinant DNA, monoclonal antibodies.

BUBBLE — Biotech (multi-cycle)

Genentech’s 1980 IPO launched biotech investing, with stock rising from $35 to $89 on its first day. The sector boomed through the 1980s, crashed in 1987, recovered, boomed again in 1991–1992, and crashed again. Biotech has seen repeated mini-bubbles because the promise is enormous but product timelines are long.

Internet / Dot-Com 1994–2000

Technology: World Wide Web, e-commerce, internet infrastructure.

BUBBLE — Dot-Com (1997–2000)

The most famous technology-driven stock bubble in modern history. The NASDAQ Composite rose from 1,000 in 1995 to 5,048 in March 2000. Companies with “.com” in their names saw absurd valuations — Pets.com, Webvan, and thousands of others with no profits traded at billions of dollars. The crash wiped out ~$5 trillion in market value. The internet itself was genuinely revolutionary; the speculation around it was not.

Artificial Intelligence 2010s–present

Technology: Large language models, deep learning, neural networks, generative AI.

DEBATED — Likely (ongoing)

NVIDIA rose from ~$360B market cap (2022) to over $3 trillion (2025–2026). AI companies attract enormous venture capital — OpenAI alone raised over $20B. Many AI startups have high valuations but little revenue. Major tech firms are spending $200B+/year combined on AI capex. Whether this is a bubble is the central question of Section 3.

3. Is the Current AI Revolution Creating a Bubble?

3.1 The Case That It Is a Bubble (Hawk View)

  • Pattern recognition. Every transformative technology in the past 300 years that attracted broad public enthusiasm produced a bubble — railways, radio, biotech, the internet. AI has all the hallmarks: a genuinely revolutionary technology, enormous capital inflows, sky-high valuations for unprofitable companies, and widespread hype.
  • Extreme valuations. NVIDIA trades at a P/E ratio well above historical semiconductor averages. AI startups are valued at multiples that assume decades of dominant growth. The Big Tech capex boom ($200B+ annually) resembles the fiber-optic buildout of the late 1990s.
  • Revenue uncertainty. Despite massive investment, revenue models for generative AI remain unproven. Consumer chatbots are largely free. Enterprise adoption has not yet generated returns that justify the capital deployed.
  • Historical frequency. Of the 18 technology epochs surveyed, 11 produced clear bubbles. The probability of any transformative technology triggering a bubble is well above 50%.

3.2 The Case That It Is Not (Dove View)

  • Fundamental demand is real. Unlike Pets.com, AI tools (GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, Midjourney) have millions of paying users and genuine productivity gains.
  • The infrastructure has utility. NVIDIA’s GPUs are production tools used for training and inference at scale by every major tech company — not speculative assets.
  • The 2022 correction already happened. Many tech stocks corrected sharply in 2022. The current bull run may represent a second, more selective wave.
  • Strong business models. Microsoft, Google, and Amazon are cash-rich incumbents integrating AI into profitable existing businesses.

3.5 Conclusion

The historical evidence strongly suggests that the current AI revolution is in bubble territory. Out of 17 previous technology epochs examined, 11 produced clear financial bubbles. The AI boom exhibits the same pattern: genuine technological breakthrough → massive capital inflows → extreme valuations → uncertain revenue timelines.

The probability that we are already in, or entering, an AI bubble is estimated at 70%. However, AI is also likely to be genuinely transformative in the long run, meaning the speculation is around when the value will materialize, not whether — which is precisely what makes the current period so similar to the early internet years.

The prudent investor’s lesson from history: the technology was real in every case above; the sky-high valuations were not.
