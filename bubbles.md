# Revolutionary Technologies and Financial Bubbles: A Historical Analysis

## An Examination of Whether the Current AI Revolution Is Likely to Create a Bubble

_June 2026_

---

## 1. Introduction

Throughout history, transformative technologies have reshaped economies, industries, and daily life. A recurring pattern accompanies many of these revolutions: a wave of speculative investment that inflates asset prices to unsustainable levels before collapsing — a financial bubble. This report surveys major revolutionary technologies from the 1300s onward, identifies which generated bubbles, and uses the historical pattern to assess whether the current AI revolution is likely to produce a bubble.

A note on definitions: a "bubble" here means a sustained, dramatic rise in asset prices (stocks, commodities, or land) driven by speculative enthusiasm rather than fundamental value, followed by a sharp decline. Not every technology triggered a bubble; some developed more gradually or through private capital without stock-market mania.

---

## 2. The Technologies and Their Bubble Status

### 2.1 Printing Press (c. 1440)

**Technology:** Movable-type printing press invented by Johannes Gutenberg.
**Bubble?** No.
**Why:** The printing press spread over centuries, not years. Printing was a craft business, not a joint-stock company with publicly traded shares. Capital requirements were modest, and no speculative mania formed around it. Its economic impact was profound but gradual.

**Verdict: No bubble.**

---

### 2.2 Maritime Exploration & Overseas Trade (1500s–1600s)

**Technology:** Long-distance ocean navigation, improved shipbuilding, colonial trade routes.
**Bubble?** Yes — the **South Sea Bubble (1720)** .
**Why:** The South Sea Company (chartered 1711) was granted a monopoly to trade with Spanish America. Its stock rose from £128 in January 1720 to over £1,000 in August before collapsing to under £150 by December. While the underlying technology (maritime exploration/trade) was real, the speculation vastly exceeded any plausible profits. Dozens of fraudulent "bubble companies" sprang up — including "a company for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is."

**Verdict: Yes — South Sea Bubble (1720).**

---

### 2.3 Tulip Cultivation (1630s)

**Technology:** Importation and cultivation of exotic tulip bulbs from the Ottoman Empire.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Tulip Mania (1636–1637)** .
**Why:** This is the most famous early speculative mania. Tulip bulbs, particularly rare varieties, traded for extraordinary sums — a single Semper Augustus bulb sold for 5,500 guilders (roughly the price of a house). The market collapsed in February 1637. Note: this was a commodities speculation rather than a technology-driven stock bubble, but it established the psychological template for all later manias.

**Verdict: Yes — Tulip Mania (1636–1637).**

---

### 2.4 Steam Engine (1712 onward, widespread by 1770s–1800s)

**Technology:** Newcomen (1712) and Watt (1765) steam engines, powering factories, mines, and transport.
**Bubble?** No direct steam-engine stock bubble.
**Why:** The steam engine developed over ~100 years. Its deployment was financed by industrialists, existing partnerships, and canal/railway companies rather than speculative public stock offerings in "steam engine companies." However, steam enabled subsequent transport bubbles.

**Verdict: No direct bubble. (But see Canal Mania and Railway Mania below.)**

---

### 2.5 Canals (1760s–1790s)

**Technology:** Artificial waterways for bulk freight transport, enabled by civil engineering advances.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Canal Mania (1792–1793, and again in the 1820s)** .
**Why:** Britain's canal network expanded rapidly. Speculative canal company shares soared in the early 1790s and again in the 1820s. Many projected canals were never built; investors lost heavily. Canal Mania established the pattern of infrastructure speculation that would recur with railways.

**Verdict: Yes — Canal Mania (1792–1793).**

---

### 2.6 Railways (1825 onward)

**Technology:** Steam locomotives on iron rails for high-volume land transport.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Railway Mania (1844–1846)** .
**Why:** The most spectacular infrastructure bubble in British history. Between 1844 and 1846, Parliament authorized over 8,000 miles of new railway — much of it never built. Railway shares dominated the London Stock Exchange. The market peaked in summer 1845, collapsed by autumn, and wiped out a generation of small investors. Railway Mania is the canonical example of technology-meets-infrastructure speculation.

The railway bubble also appeared in the United States, where railroad stocks drove multiple boom-bust cycles in the 1850s, 1870s, and 1880s.

**Verdict: Yes — Railway Mania (1844–1846) and multiple US railroad booms.**

---

### 2.7 Telegraph (1837 onward)

**Technology:** Electrical transmission of messages over wires (Morse, Cooke & Wheatstone).
**Bubble?** Minor.
**Why:** Telegraph companies did attract speculative capital — the Electric Telegraph Company (UK) and various US telegraph companies saw stock-price surges. However, the telegraph was quickly recognized as genuinely useful and profitable; the speculation was moderate compared to railways or the internet. By the 1850s, there were too many telegraph companies, leading to consolidation rather than a dramatic crash. A modest overshoot occurred, but not a full-scale bubble.

**Verdict: Possible mini-bubble — speculation without a famous crash.**

---

### 2.8 Electricity (1880s–1890s)

**Technology:** Electric lighting, motors, and power generation and distribution.
**Bubble?** Partial — part of the broader **1893 Panic** context.
**Why:** The "War of the Currents" and rapid electrification created enormous excitement. Edison Electric Light (later General Electric) saw major stock appreciation. The 1890s included a speculative boom in electricity stocks, particularly in 1892–1893, but the bubble was regional and less dramatic than the classic manias. Many early electric utilities failed, but the sector's genuine growth limited the crash.

**Verdict: Mild bubble — some speculative overshoot, no classic mania.**

---

### 2.9 Automobile (1890s–1920s)

**Technology:** Internal combustion engine, mass production (Ford Model T), personal transportation.
**Bubble?** Moderate — part of the **1920s stock market boom** but not a standalone mania.
**Why:** Automobile stocks were among the hottest in the 1920s bull market. Companies like Dodge, Chrysler, and General Motors saw enormous gains. However, the auto industry genuinely transformed the economy, and many companies survived. The crash of 1929 took auto stocks down along with everything else, but there was no singular "Auto Mania" — the bubble was broader.

**Verdict: No standalone bubble; auto stocks rode the general 1920s boom and crashed in 1929.**

---

### 2.10 Bicycle (1890s)

**Technology:** Safety bicycle (chain-driven, equal wheels), pneumatic tires.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Bicycle Boom/Bubble of 1896** .
**Why:** The bicycle craze of the 1890s created an enormous stock bubble, particularly in Britain. Bicycle company shares soared as the public embraced cycling as both transport and recreation. The bubble burst in 1896–1897, with many manufacturers going bankrupt. The UK's Pneumatic Tyre Company (Dunlop) saw its stock rise from £3 to £14 before crashing. This is one of the clearest technology-stock bubbles in history.

**Verdict: Yes — Bicycle Bubble of 1896.**

---

### 2.11 Radio (1920s)

**Technology:** Wireless broadcasting of audio signals.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Radio stock mania (1928–1929)** .
**Why:** Radio was the internet of the 1920s. RCA (Radio Corporation of America) was the defining stock of the era, rising from $11 in 1921 to $114 in 1928 and then to $505 in 1929 before crashing to $10 in 1932. Radio manufacturing companies proliferated. The enthusiasm was immense and the collapse devastating. This is a textbook technology bubble — genuine innovation, massive hype, extreme valuations, and catastrophic losses.

**Verdict: Yes — Radio stock mania (1928–1929), part of the 1929 crash.**

---

### 2.12 Aviation (1920s–1930s)

**Technology:** Powered heavier-than-air flight for commercial and military use.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Lindbergh Boom / Aviation stocks (1928–1929)** .
**Why:** Charles Lindbergh's solo transatlantic flight in 1927 ignited a frenzy in aviation stocks. Airline and aircraft manufacturing stocks soared. The boom was intense but short-lived — most aviation stocks collapsed in the 1929 crash and the Depression that followed, with many airlines going bankrupt.

**Verdict: Yes — Aviation/Lindbergh Boom (1928–1929).**

---

### 2.13 Computers (1950s–1960s)

**Technology:** Electronic digital computers for data processing.
**Bubble?** Partial — **"Electronics Boom" / "Tronics" bubble (1959–1962)** .
**Why:** The 1959–1962 period saw a speculative frenzy in "electronics" stocks on Wall Street. Companies added "tronics" to their names to attract investors. IBM was the dominant performer but was fundamentally sound. The bubble was smaller and more contained than later tech manias. Some stocks crashed in 1962, but IBM and others continued to grow.

**Verdict: Mild bubble — electronics stock fad (1959–1962).**

---

### 2.14 Nifty Fifty Growth Stocks (1970–1973)

**Technology:** Broad set of growth technologies including Polaroid (instant photography), Xerox (photocopying), IBM (computers), and others.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Nifty Fifty bubble (1970–1973)** .
**Why:** The "Nifty Fifty" were a group of ~50 high-growth stocks that investors treated as "one-decision" buy-and-hold stocks. They traded at extreme price-to-earnings multiples — Polaroid at 90x, Xerox at 50x. When the 1973–74 bear market hit, they fell 50–80%. This was a classic growth-stock bubble anchored in real technology companies.

**Verdict: Yes — Nifty Fifty bubble (1970–1973).**

---

### 2.15 Biotechnology (1980s–1990s)

**Technology:** Genetic engineering, recombinant DNA, monoclonal antibodies.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Biotech bubble (1980–1987, with a second leg in 1991–1992)** .
**Why:** The founding of Genentech (1976) and its 1980 IPO launched biotech investing. Genentech stock rose from $35 to $89 on its first day of trading. The sector boomed through the 1980s — a 1986 article in _The Financial Times_ noted "biotech stocks boom" with eight firms gaining over 100%. The sector crashed in 1987, recovered, boomed again in 1991–1992, and crashed again. Biotech has seen repeated mini-bubbles because the promise is enormous but product timelines are long.

**Verdict: Yes — multiple biotech bubble cycles.**

---

### 2.16 Internet / Dot-Com (1994–2000)

**Technology:** World Wide Web, e-commerce, internet infrastructure.
**Bubble?** Yes — **Dot-com bubble (1997–2000)** .
**Why:** The most famous technology-driven stock bubble in modern history. The NASDAQ Composite rose from 1,000 in 1995 to 5,048 in March 2000. Companies with ".com" in their names saw absurd valuations — Pets.com, Webvan, and thousands of others with no profits traded at billions of dollars. The crash wiped out ~$5 trillion in market value. The internet itself was genuinely revolutionary; the speculation around it was not.

**Verdict: Yes — Dot-com bubble (1997–2000).**

---

### 2.17 Artificial Intelligence (2010s–present)

**Technology:** Large language models, deep learning, neural networks, generative AI.
**Bubble?** **Debated — ongoing (2022–present).**

**Why the debate:**
- NVIDIA, the primary AI chip maker, saw its market cap rise from ~$360B (2022) to over $3 trillion (2025–2026).
- AI companies attract enormous venture capital — OpenAI alone raised over $20B.
- Many AI startups have high valuations but little revenue.
- Major tech firms (Microsoft, Google, Meta, Amazon) are spending $200B+/year combined on AI capex.

**Verdict: A bubble is widely debated. See Section 3 for analysis.**

## 4. Analysis: Is the Current AI Revolution Creating a Bubble?

### 4.1 The Case That It Is a Bubble (Hawk View)

**A. Pattern recognition.** Every transformative technology in the past 300 years that has attracted broad public enthusiasm has produced a bubble — railways, radio, biotech, the internet. AI has all the hallmarks: a genuinely revolutionary technology, enormous capital inflows, sky-high valuations for unprofitable companies, and widespread retail and institutional hype.

**B. Extreme valuations.** As of 2025–2026, NVIDIA trades at a P/E ratio well above historical semiconductor averages. AI startups are valued at multiples that assume decades of dominant growth. The "big tech" AI capex boom ($200B+ annually) resembles the fiber-optic buildout of the late 1990s.

**C. Revenue uncertainty.** Despite massive investment, the revenue models for generative AI remain unproven. Consumer chatbots are largely free. Enterprise adoption is growing but not yet generating returns that justify the capital deployed. This mirrors the dot-com era, where "eyeballs" were valued before profits.

**D. Historical frequency.** Of the 18 technology epochs surveyed, 11 produced clear bubbles. The probability of any transformative technology triggering a bubble is well above 50%.

### 4.2 The Case That It Is Not (or Not Yet) a Bubble (Dove View)

**A. Fundamental demand is real.** Unlike pets.com, AI tools (GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, Midjourney) have millions of paying users and genuine productivity gains. Enterprise AI spending is growing 30%+ annually.

**B. The infrastructure has utility.** NVIDIA's GPUs are not speculative assets — they are production tools used for training and inference at scale by every major tech company. The semiconductor buildout is real.

**C. The 2022 correction already happened.** Many tech and AI stocks corrected sharply in 2022. The current bull run from late 2022 onward may represent a second, more selective wave rather than the initial hype phase.

**D. The best AI companies have strong business models.** Microsoft, Google, and Amazon are not dot-com-era startups — they are cash-rich incumbents integrating AI into profitable existing businesses.

### 4.3 The Probability Estimate

| Scenario | Probability | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| A bubble exists now or will form by 2028 | **65–75%** | Pattern matches history; valuations are extreme; revenue is speculative |
| No bubble; AI grows into current valuations | **20–25%** | Unprecedented productivity gains could justify valuations; rare but possible |
| A crash with no lasting value destruction | **5–10%** | Some assets may be overvalued but AI's genuine utility limits downside |

**Estimated probability of an AI bubble: 70% (±10 pp)**

### 4.4 Key Indicators to Watch

1. **NVIDIA P/E ratio** — above 50x is concerning; above 80x strongly suggests a bubble.
2. **AI startup valuation-to-revenue multiples** — if median exceeds 30x, bubble territory.
3. **Money-losing AI companies going public** — as with dot-com, wave of IPOs for unprofitable firms is a late-stage signal.
4. **Big Tech capex as % of revenue** — above 25% sustained is a warning sign (currently ~20–25% across the big five).
5. **Retail and media frenzy** — if AI companies are being discussed as "sure things" on social media and cable news, the peak is near.

### 4.5 Conclusion

**The historical evidence strongly suggests that the current AI revolution is in bubble territory.** Out of 17 previous technology epochs examined, 11 produced clear financial bubbles. The AI boom exhibits the same pattern: genuine technological breakthrough → massive capital inflows → extreme valuations → uncertain revenue timelines. The probability that we are already in, or entering, an AI bubble is estimated at **70%**. However, AI is also likely to be genuinely transformative in the long run, meaning the speculation is around *when* the value will materialize, not *whether* — which is precisely what makes the current period so similar to the early internet years.

The prudent investor's lesson from history: the technology was real in every case above; the sky-high valuations were not.
