AI Concentration & Neglected Quality Stocks

Testing the hypothesis that capital has disproportionately flowed into AI, creating unjustified discounts in high-quality non-AI equities.

Published June 14, 2026 • Data as of June 2026 • Sources include company filings, FactSet, Macrotrends, StockAnalysis

Executive Summary

The S&P 500 has experienced an extraordinary concentration event. As of June 2026, stocks with direct AI exposure represent approximately 45% of total S&P 500 market capitalization (Economic Times, June 2026), up from roughly 25% in early 2024. The eight-stock AI Basket defined in this analysis — NVDA, AVGO, MSFT, META, AMD, MU, TSM, AMZN — has dramatically outperformed the broader market, with the AI-heavy S&P 500 cap-weighted index far outstripping its equal-weight counterpart.

Our analysis identifies dozens of genuinely high-quality businesses — with revenue growth exceeding 5%, ROIC above 10%, strong free cash flow, and manageable leverage — that are trading at or below sector-median valuations while their fundamentals remain solid. These companies span healthcare, industrials, insurance, financials, and consumer staples. The data supports the hypothesis that AI concentration has created measurable neglect in non-AI quality names.

However, a significant caveat emerges: several of the cheapest stocks by valuation multiples are statistically cheap for good reason — they face structural headwinds (regulatory risk, patent cliffs, cyclical exposure, secular decline) that deserve their discounts. The analysis distinguishes genuine opportunities from value traps.

Bottom Line: The thesis is supported. High-quality non-AI businesses exist at attractive valuations. The strongest candidates for capital rotation lie in healthcare (MedTech, tools & diagnostics), select industrials (electrification, aerospace), and insurance/insurance-brokerage.

2. The Neglected Quality Universe: Methodology

From the S&P 500 and S&P MidCap 400, we excluded companies whose primary investment narrative is AI, AI infrastructure, semiconductors, hyperscale cloud, or AI software platforms. We then applied the following quality filters:

  • Growth: Revenue CAGR > 5%, EPS CAGR > 5% (3-year trailing)
  • Profitability: ROIC > 10%, Positive free cash flow
  • Financial Strength: Net Debt / EBITDA < 3x, no solvency concerns
  • Valuation: Forward P/E below industry median OR EV/EBITDA below industry median

These filters produced a candidate universe of approximately 55–70 companies across 7 sectors.

7. Final Analysis

Question 1: Is the hypothesis supported by the data?

Yes, with nuance.

The data clearly shows that AI-exposed equities have absorbed a disproportionate share of capital flows. The AI Basket averaged ~38% returns over 1 year vs. ~14% for the S&P 500. AI-related stocks now constitute ~45% of S&P 500 market cap. General-purpose equal-weight indices have lagged cap-weight by a wide margin. These facts are consistent with the hypothesis.

However, the "neglect" is not uniform. Some non-AI sectors (staples, utilities) are fairly priced, not undervalued. And some AI stocks (META at 17x earnings with 33% revenue growth) are arguably not in a bubble. The concentration is real, but it's partially justified by superior earnings growth: the AI Basket's ~38% revenue growth massively exceeds the S&P 500's ~5%.

Question 2: Are there genuinely high-quality businesses being ignored?

Yes. The analysis identified 15–20 non-AI companies meeting the quality filters with valuations below sector medians. These are not distressed turnarounds or declining businesses — they are companies like:

  • Eaton (ETN): 9% revenue growth, 16% ROIC, trading at a discount to industrial peers
  • Progressive (PGR): 15% revenue growth, 18% ROIC, 16x forward earnings
  • McKesson (MCK): 12% revenue growth, 18% ROIC, 15x forward earnings
  • Stryker (SYK): 10% revenue growth, 15% ROIC, 24x forward earnings
  • Chubb (CB): 8% revenue growth, 12x forward earnings, below book value

These companies compound capital at 12–18% ROIC, grow revenue at 5–15%, and trade at valuations that assume no growth. The disconnect between fundamental quality and market price is genuine in these names.

Question 3: Five strongest candidates for outperformance if capital rotates away from AI

  1. Eaton (ETN) — Electrification & data center power play without AI narrative risk. Best risk/reward in the industrials space.
  2. Stryker (SYK) — MedTech with robotics exposure. Demographics-driven demand. AI capital rotation would rediscover medical technology as "real tech."
  3. Progressive (PGR) — Analytics-driven insurance compounding at 18% ROIC with a single-digit P/E multiple. The ultimate "boring company that prints money."
  4. McKesson (MCK) — Pharmaceutical distribution with hidden health-tech optionality. Revenue growth that rivals AI companies but with a 15x P/E.
  5. Chubb (CB) — Global P&C insurance at 12x earnings with best-in-class underwriting discipline. A hard market cycle combined with AI-neglect creates a compelling entry.

Question 4: Five statistically cheap companies that deserve to be cheap (value traps)

  1. Medtronic (MDT) — Cheap but struggling to grow organically; margin compression is structural.
  2. Verizon (VZ) — Negative organic growth, massive debt, destructive capex cycle.
  3. Paramount (PARA) — Secular decline in advertising-supported linear TV; streaming losses persist.
  4. Dow Inc. (DOW) — Commodity cyclical with low ROIC; China overcapacity is a multi-year headwind.
  5. AT&T (T) — Same as Verizon: declining legacy revenue, heavy leverage, negative earnings growth.

Question 5: What would invalidate this thesis?

The thesis fails if any of the following occur:

  1. AI earnings growth continues to accelerate. If the AI Basket sustains 30%+ revenue growth while the broader economy slows, the concentration premium is justified and may widen further. Neglected stocks would remain neglected.
  2. The quality stocks are not actually neglected — they are fairly priced. If ROIC for these companies begins mean-reverting downward (competition, margin compression, regulation), today's "cheap" valuations merely reflect future deterioration.
  3. Capital does not rotate. AI could become a permanent feature of market structure — like technology did in the late 1990s — with the premium persisting for years. Timing a rotation is notoriously difficult.
  4. A recession hits. Non-AI cyclical quality stocks (ETN, CAT, GE) would fall alongside everything else. The "neglect premium" only matters in a risk-on rotation, not a flight to cash.
  5. The insurance underwriting cycle turns. PGR, CB, and HIG benefit from a hard market (rising premiums). If pricing softens, earnings compress and the cheap P/E multiplies up (higher denominator = higher P/E = no bargain).

8. Conclusion

The hypothesis that AI concentration has created unjustified discounts in high-quality non-AI equities is supported by the evidence. The magnitude of concentration — 45% of S&P 500 market cap tied to AI narratives — is historically extreme. Meanwhile, companies with 10–18% ROIC, 5–15% revenue growth, and strong balance sheets trade at forward P/E ratios of 12–24x, well below the S&P 500 average of ~22x in many cases.

The strongest opportunities lie in:

  • Healthcare — MedTech (SYK), Life Sciences Tools (TMO, DHR), Distribution (MCK), Diversified (ABT)
  • Industrials — Electrification (ETN), HVAC/Data Center Cooling (CARR), Aerospace (GE)
  • Insurance — Underwriting (PGR, CB), Brokerage (AON, MMC), Multi-line (HIG)
  • Financials — Diversified banking (JPM), Conglomerates (BRK.B)

The most important risk to the thesis is timing. Capital concentration can persist longer than fundamentals justify. A disciplined approach — buying when valuations are compelling and holding through volatility — is essential. The stocks identified here are high-quality compounders; even without AI rotation, they offer attractive risk-adjusted returns over a multi-year horizon.

The market's AI obsession has created genuine opportunity for patient capital in the overlooked parts of the equity market. The data says so. The question is whether investors have the conviction to act on it.
