Why the Stock Could Underperform

1. The "Electrification Super-Cycle" Narrative Is Peaking

The bull case for ETN rests on a once-in-a-generation electrification capex wave: data centers, EV charging infrastructure, grid modernization, and re-shoring. It's a compelling story. It's also exactly the kind of narrative that lures investors in just before the cycle turns.

Data center capex is the most vulnerable leg. The bull case assumes hyperscaler spending on AI infrastructure continues growing at 20%+ for years. But the history of technology capex cycles is clear: they overshoot, then correct. In 2023, the "Magnificent Seven" alone spent ~$180B on capex. By 2026, that number is pushing $300B+. At some point — likely when AI revenue fails to materialize at the promised scale — these companies will cut. Meta alone cut its 2023 capex guidance by $4B after a single earnings call in 2022. When hyperscalers pull back, ETN's electrical segment — which gets a disproportionate valuation premium for the "data center growth story" — takes the biggest multiple hit.

Commercial construction is already slowing. US non-residential construction spending, excluding manufacturing, has been decelerating through 2025. Office vacancy rates remain at all-time highs (~20% nationally). Higher-for-longer interest rates are crushing the economics of new commercial development. ETN's electrical business generates roughly 35–40% of revenue from commercial construction exposure. If this segment contracts even 5%, the margin impact is amplified by operating leverage — and ETN's 20%+ segment margins on electrical are at cyclical highs.

2. The Multiple Is the Problem

ETN trades at ~28x forward earnings. That's a 45% premium to its 10-year average of ~19x. The bull case says this is justified by the electrification super-cycle. The bear case says it's an industrial company trading like a growth tech stock with none of the recurring revenue characteristics.

For context, Emerson Electric (EMR) — a comparable industrial — trades at ~22x. Rockwell Automation (ROK) at ~21x. Schneider Electric trades at ~24x in Europe. ETN's 28x is an outlier. If the multiple simply reverts to 22x (still above its historical average), the stock declines 21% even if earnings meet expectations.

3. Competitive Threats Are Ignored

The bull case assumes ETN's electrical distribution and power management franchise is unassailable. It's not. ABB and Siemens are making aggressive pushes into the same data center and electrification markets. Chinese competitors like Chint and Delixi are expanding in low-voltage electrical equipment globally. Meanwhile, the shift toward solid-state transformers and silicon-carbide power electronics could disrupt the traditional electrical equipment hierarchy faster than incumbents expect. ETN's R&D spend as a % of revenue (~3.5%) is below the industrial peer average.

When the cycle turns, both earnings and multiple compress simultaneously — the classic "double hit" that turns a 10% earnings miss into a 35% stock decline.

Why the Stock Could Underperform

1. Mako Is Great. Everyone Else Has Mako Now.

The bull case revolves around Stryker's Mako robotic-assisted surgery platform — and with good reason. Mako is the dominant orthopedic robotics platform with ~1,500 installed systems. It drives instrument and implant attachment, creates switching costs for surgeons, and justifies a premium multiple.

The problem: competition is closing faster than Stryker's lead is widening. Johnson & Johnson's VELYs robotic system has now completed over 100,000 procedures globally and is installed in ~500 sites. Smith+Nephew's CORI system is gaining traction. Zimmer Biomet's Rosa platform, while clunkier, is bundled into their hip and knee portfolio. Most critically, hospitals are pushing back — they don't want five different robotic systems in their ORs, and they're using the competitive dynamic to force price concessions. Mako's attach rate premium (the extra implant pricing Stryker can command from Mako-enabled procedures) is shrinking. In 2023, Mako-enabled knees carried a ~15% pricing premium. By 2025, early data suggested that premium was down to 8–10%.

2. Orthopedics Is a Mature Market With Structural Pricing Pressure

Global orthopedic device market growth has slowed from ~5% annually (2015–2019) to ~3.5% (2022–2025). The reason: hospital consolidation is shifting bargaining power away from device manufacturers. In the US, the top 10 hospital systems now control ~25% of beds, up from ~15% a decade ago. These systems have dedicated supply chain teams running competitive RFPs. They are demanding bundled pricing across product categories. Stryker's "med surg" business (hospital equipment, beds, stretchers) faces even more aggressive procurement pressure.

Medicare reimbursement cuts are coming. The CMS Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) final rule for 2025 cut reimbursement for several joint replacement procedures. If the US moves toward site-neutral payments or reference pricing for orthopedic implants — a bipartisan idea that's been discussed — Stryker's implant pricing power would be structurally impaired.

3. The Neurotechnology/M&A Growth Story Has a Validation Problem

Stryker's neurotechnology segment (acquired through the $3B + various bolt-on acquisitions) is supposed to be the next growth engine. But neuromodulation markets have repeatedly disappointed. Medtronic's deep brain stimulation franchise — the largest in the world — has grown at only 4–5% annually. Stryker's neuro segment is still generating negative free cash flow. The bull case assumes it becomes a material earnings contributor by 2028. The bear case says it's a science project that will eventually be written down.

Why the Stock Could Underperform

1. The Auto Insurance Cycle Is Turning — and It Always Destroys Earnings

Progressive is having an exceptional run. The combination of rate increases taken through 2023–2025 (cumulative ~25–30%) and moderating claims cost inflation has produced combined ratios in the low 90s — excellent profitability. But this is the peak of the cycle, not the beginning.

Here's how the auto insurance cycle works when it turns: (1) Industry profitability improves → (2) Competitors see the returns and start adding capacity → (3) Rate increases stop, then reverse → (4) Loss ratios deteriorate as prior-year pricing proves inadequate → (5) Profits collapse. We are at step 2–3 right now. Geico has already begun advertising "low rates" again after years of restraint. Allstate is adding auto insurance capacity after pulling back. State Farm, which lost $8B in auto in 2022, has written its way back to profitability and is pricing aggressively again. The competitive discipline that existed from 2021–2024 is gone.

Progressive's own guidance suggests combined ratios will drift from ~92 in 2025 to ~95+ in 2026 as prior-year rate adequacy erodes. Every point of combined ratio deterioration represents approximately $350M in pre-tax profit — or about $0.50 in EPS. A move from 92 to 97 (still profitable, but not exceptional) would reduce EPS by ~$2.50 — a 17% earnings decline from consensus.

2. Telematics Advantage Is Narrowing

Progressive's "secret weapon" has been its 20-year head start on usage-based insurance (Snapshot/telematics). This data advantage let it price risk more accurately than peers, segment good drivers from bad, and maintain a claims cost advantage of 5–10% over competitors.

That advantage is eroding. Geico launched its telematics program (DriveEasy) and now has over 5 million enrolled drivers. Allstate's Arity platform aggregates data from 80M+ cars. State Farm's Drive Safe & Save (partnered with GM's OnStar) covers millions. Even upstarts like Root and Lemonade, while small, are commoditizing the data layer. The insurance data that was Progressive's proprietary moat is increasingly available to everyone through connected car APIs, smartphone sensors, and third-party data aggregators. Progressive's loss ratio advantage over peers has narrowed from ~8 points in 2019 to an estimated ~4 points in 2025.

3. Catastrophe Exposure Is Underestimated

Progressive has been growing its homeowners insurance business aggressively — direct premiums written in homeowners grew from $2.5B in 2020 to over $5B in 2025. This is a completely different risk profile from auto insurance. Homeowners is catastrophe-exposed in ways that Progressive's models may not fully capture — especially given climate change-driven loss volatility. Florida homeowners alone generated $40B+ of insured losses from 2020–2024. Progressive's homeowners combined ratio (estimated ~105 in 2025) is worse than its auto book. They're adding low-margin, high-volatility exposure that could generate a $500M–$1B loss in a single bad hurricane season.

The bull case says "insurance cycle + float income." The bear case says "you are paying 19x earnings for a company whose core competitive advantage is eroding, entering a peak cycle, and adding catastrophe risk to a previously stable book."

Why the Stock Could Underperform

1. The Opioid Liability Is Not Over — It's Just Starting

The bull case says the opioid settlement framework (McKesson agreed to pay ~$7.4B over 18 years) removes the legal overhang. This is dangerously naive. The opioid litigation is entering a second, more dangerous phase: public nuisance and abatement trials at the state and local level. The settlement McKesson signed covers claims against the company as a distributor — but new abatement demands, "public nuisance" lawsuits, and litigation from states that opted out of the national settlement could add billions more.

Consider: In 2024, West Virginia's trial against McKesson and other distributors resulted in a $383M verdict. That's a state with 1.8M people. There are hundreds of counties, cities, and Native American tribes pursuing their own claims. The $7.4B settlement is a ceiling, not a floor. Every additional $1B in opioid-related payments represents roughly $7 per share in NPV destruction.

2. Drug Pricing Reform Is the Unseen Iceberg

McKesson makes money by earning a percentage spread on the drugs it distributes. The margin is thin (~1–2% of revenue), but the dollar volume is enormous. Anything that compresses this spread is existential.

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) drug pricing provisions are just the beginning. The IRA allows Medicare to negotiate prices on 10 drugs initially, expanding to 20+ by 2029. While this directly affects manufacturers, the ripple effects compress margins throughout the supply chain. Distributors face pressure from both sides: manufacturers demanding lower distribution fees (as their own margins shrink) and pharmacies demanding lower drug costs (as patients face lower reimbursements).

De minimis safe harbor for pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) is under threat. The proposed rule from HHS/CMS could eliminate the regulatory structure that allows PBMs and distributors to profit from drug rebates. If the PBM model is restructured (a bipartisan idea that keeps gaining momentum), McKesson's Revenue Management Solutions and other fee-based services take a direct hit. PBM reform could reduce McKesson's EPS by $1–2.

3. The "Value Trap" Triple Threat

McKesson trades at 15x earnings with a 1% dividend yield and massive buyback activity. This looks cheap. But the market is right to be skeptical.

The company has spent $15B+ on share buybacks over the last 5 years, reducing the share count by ~25%. This creates the illusion of EPS growth. But organic revenue growth in distribution is only 4–6% annually — barely above inflation. Operating margins in the core distribution business are ~1.5%. The buyback-driven EPS growth is a financial engineering game, not a sustainable compounding story. At some point, the buybacks deplete and the real earnings growth — or lack thereof — is exposed.

Compare to Cardinal Health (CAH) and AmerisourceBergen (COR): All three trade at similar multiples (14–16x). None has been able to sustainably grow operating margins. The entire distribution sector trades cheap for a reason — it's a high-volume, low-margin business with limited pricing power. McKesson's "transformation" into a healthcare services company is a self-serving narrative that has failed to meaningfully move the margin needle for a decade.

Why the Stock Could Underperform

1. The Hard Market Is Already Priced In — and It's Softening

Chubb has benefited enormously from the P&C hard market of 2020–2025. Commercial insurance rates increased 60%+ cumulatively over this period. Chubb's premium growth and underwriting margins have been outstanding. The bull case assumes this continues.

It won't. The P&C cycle is turning. Commercial lines pricing increases have already decelerated from +12% in 2023 to +3–5% in 2025 according to the Council of Insurance Agents and Brokers (CIAB) quarterly survey. In casualty lines — Chubb's core strength — pricing is actually declining in competitive classes. The industry is adding capacity: new entrants and existing carriers are chasing premium dollars after years of profitability. When pricing turns negative — a "soft market" — Chubb's growth decelerates and its loss ratio deteriorates as reserves booked on peak-cycle premiums prove inadequate.

Chubb generates ~$14B of underwriting income at the peak of the cycle. In a soft market, that number could fall to $6–8B. The math is simple: 12x earnings at the peak is not cheap — it's fair. 12x earnings at the trough is expensive.

2. Catastrophe Losses Are a Structural Problem Masquerading as Transient

Chubb has significant exposure to property catastrophe risk through its global P&C book. The company has been growing its North American property book aggressively, attracted by the post-2022 rate increases in cat-exposed lines. But climate change is structurally increasing both the frequency and severity of natural catastrophes.

The facts: Insured catastrophe losses have exceeded $100B in each of the last three years (2023–2025), compared to a long-term average of ~$50B. Chubb's annual catastrophe losses have run at $1.5–2.5B, but the trend is rising. A single $10B cat event (Florida hurricane or California earthquake) could hit Chubb's book for $1–2B of pre-tax losses — wiping out 15–25% of annual operating earnings.

Reinsurance costs are the hidden tax. Even when catastrophe losses are manageable, the cost of buying reinsurance protection is structurally higher. Chubb's reinsurance spend has increased ~30–40% from 2021 levels, and those costs are not fully recoverable through higher primary rates in competitive markets. This is a permanent impairment to underwriting margins.

3. Interest Rate Exposure Cuts Both Ways

The bull case highlights that higher interest rates boost Chubb's investment income on its massive bond portfolio (~$95B). This is partially true: Chubb is earning ~4.5% on new money versus ~2.5% on the legacy portfolio, creating a tailwind as bonds mature and roll into higher rates.

But interest rates are also Chubb's single biggest latent liability. Chubb holds a massive duration mismatch: its bond portfolio has a duration of ~4 years (meaning rates can't change too much without significant mark-to-market loss), while its insurance liabilities extend 5–10+ years. If the Federal Reserve cuts rates (as markets expect in 2026–2027), Chubb's reinvestment yield falls, compressing the margin between investment income and claims cost inflation. A 100bp rate cut reduces Chubb's investment income by ~$500M annually — or ~$1.25 per share.

Furthermore, Chubb holds ~$6B in bond portfolio unrealized losses from the 2022–2023 rate increase. While these are amortized, they represent real economic capital that constrains Chubb's ability to deploy excess capital for buybacks when operating conditions are best.

  • Reinsurance renewal rate increase reverses: A decline in reinsurance costs would signal that capacity is returning — bad for primary pricing.

Bear Verdict on CB: The market is correctly discounting peak-cycle earnings. A 12x multiple on a business at the peak of the hardest insurance market in 20 years is not a bargain — it's market efficiency. When earnings revert to mid-cycle, the stock will be 20–30% lower.

Final Synthesis — When "Neglected" Means "Rightly Ignored"

The premise of this series was that the market has overlooked a basket of high-quality, well-managed businesses because investors are obsessed with AI stocks. The bull case says these five stocks trade at attractive valuations for their durable competitive advantages and are poised for a re-rating as attention returns to fundamentals.

The bear case says something more uncomfortable: maybe the market is not wrong.

You can construct a compelling bull narrative for each of these stocks using optimistic assumptions about cycles, competitive positions, and multiple expansion. But the bear case for every single one of them rests on the same pattern: buying peak-cycle earnings at a price that's only attractive if the cycle never turns. ETN's 28x multiple assumes the electrification super-cycle has no end. PGR's 19x assumes auto insurance margins stay at generational highs. CB's 12x assumes hard market pricing never softens. MCK's 15x assumes buybacks can sustain earnings growth forever.

These are all cycle-peak valuations. And cycles turn.

Which Bear Arguments Are Strongest Overall?

The three most credible bear cases across all five stocks:

#1: The Auto Insurance Cycle Turn (PGR)
This is the most predictable bear case in the basket. The auto insurance cycle has turned every 4–7 years for 50+ years. We are at the peak. PGR's combined ratio will drift from 92 to 96+ as rate adequacy erodes. Every other auto insurer is adding capacity. The telematics advantage is narrowing. And PGR's valuation still assumes peak-cycle earnings are permanent. This is not a question of if — it's when. The only variable is magnitude.

#2: The Industrial Multiple Trap (ETN)
ETN is a fine business at 19x earnings. At 28x, it is pricing in uninterrupted perfection for a company that sells electrical components into a cyclical end market. Valuing ETN at 28x is like valuing an auto supplier at 25x in 2018 or a homebuilder at 15x in 2005. The historical evidence is overwhelming: industrial companies at >25x forward earnings almost never deliver positive returns over the subsequent 3 years.

#3: The Hidden Catastrophe Tax (CB & PGR)
Both insurers are adding catastrophe-exposed business in an era of structurally elevated disaster losses. Reinsurance costs are permanently higher. Climate change is not a transient phenomenon — it's a secular trend. Insurers that grew through hard market rate increases in cat-exposed lines are going to face a material headwind as loss costs rise faster than pricing. This is the most underappreciated risk across the entire basket.

What Would Invalidate the Entire "Neglected Quality" Thesis?

The bear cases laid out in this article are individual stock-level arguments. But there's a deeper question: if 3 or more of these 5 stocks underperform, what does that say about the original "neglected quality" hypothesis?

The answer is uncomfortable. If the bear cases for PGR, ETN, and MCK all prove correct — producing 20–40% declines — it would suggest the market is not neglecting these stocks. It is rationally discounting them for reasons that the "valuation at face value" screening methodology missed.

Consider what all five stocks share:

  • Cyclical exposure that a static screening methodology cannot capture. The quality screening (ROIC, margins, debt, stability) is backward-looking. It identifies businesses with good past performance, but tells you nothing about where they are in their cycle. All five are at or near peak cycle conditions.
  • Industry structures that look durable but are being disrupted. Orthopedics looks like an oligopoly, but hospital consolidation is shifting power. Insurance looks like a data moat, but telematics is being commoditized. Drug distribution looks like an unassailable middleman, but PBM reform threatens the model.
  • Low multiples that are actually fair, not cheap. A common investor mistake: assuming a stock trading below the market multiple is "undervalued." When a stock trades at 12–15x in an S&P 500 that's at 21x, the market is telling you something. The market prices a discount for risk. Ignoring that risk signal is not "contrarian" — it's naive.

The Uncomfortable Conclusion

What if these stocks aren't neglected? What if they're correctly priced?

The market does sometimes make mistakes. But when five stocks in unrelated industries all appear "cheap" by the same metric, the error is more likely in the metric than in the collective wisdom of millions of market participants. The "neglected quality" thesis assumes the market is miscalculating the risk. The bear case says the market has correctly calculated the risk, and the risk is higher than the screen suggests.

We believe the bear case is more likely than the bull case for at least 3 of these 5 stocks.

This doesn't mean the "neglected quality" concept is worthless. It means it needs refinement. The right conclusion is probably not "buy all five equally." It's more nuanced:

  • Stocks at cycle peaks should not be bought. PGR and ETN belong here. Their earnings power is deteriorating, not stable.
  • Stocks with secular overhangs should only be bought at a deep discount. MCK and CB belong here. 15x is not deep enough for the regulatory and catastrophe risks.
  • The highest-quality name in the basket is the best bet — but at a lower entry. SYK has the strongest competitive position and the most durable growth driver (demographics). But even SYK needs to be 15–20% lower to offer a proper margin of safety.

The best trade in this basket today may be to wait. All five stocks are vulnerable to the same macro trigger (recession, AI capex disappointment, or insurance cycle turn). When the sell-off comes, buy the strongest names (SYK, CB) at distressed levels. The "neglected quality" thesis works — but only as a buy the dip strategy, not as a buy the peak strategy.

One Chart to Watch

The US 10-Year Treasury Yield. More than any single company metric, the 10-year yield determines the fate of this basket. Why? Because all five stocks look attractive relative to bonds at current low absolute yields. If the 10-year rises to 5.5%+ (as it threatened in 2025), the equity risk premium on these stocks collapses. ETN's 3.2% earnings yield looks terrible next to a 5.5% risk-free yield. PGR's 5.2% earnings yield offers inadequate compensation for the cycle risk. The entire "quality at a reasonable price" trade works when rates are low or falling. If rates stay elevated — the most likely scenario given persistent fiscal deficits and services inflation — these stocks face structural headwinds from competition with bonds.
