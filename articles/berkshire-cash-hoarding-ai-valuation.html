The $325 Billion Question: Is Buffett Selling Into AI Euphoria?

📅 June 14, 2026 • 📊 Data-Driven Analysis • ⏱ 12 min read

The Core Thesis to Test

Berkshire Hathaway is sitting on a record $325+ billion in cash and Treasury bills — more than the GDP of Finland or the entire market cap of Netflix. The company has sold roughly $150+ billion in equities over the past 18 months, concentrated in the same sectors that have been supercharged by AI enthusiasm.

Is Warren Buffett — who missed the dot-com boom, missed the meme-stock frenzy, and watched his cash pile look foolish before each crash vindicated him — once again signaling that the party has gone too far? Or are there simpler, more mundane explanations that make this a false alarm?

1. The Mountain of Cash

Berkshire Hathaway's cash and Treasury bill holdings have ballooned to extraordinary levels. As of Berkshire's most recent quarterly filing (Q3 fiscal, released November 2025), the company held approximately $325 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term Treasury investments. This represents the largest cash position in the history of American corporate finance — public or private.

The pattern is clear: Berkshire sold most heavily into sectors trading at the highest premiums to historical valuation. The correlation between valuation premium and AI exposure is no coincidence — these are the same sectors. The three sectors where Berkshire made its largest reductions (consumer tech/Apple, banking with fintech AI tailwinds, cloud/Snowflake) all show elevated multiples with high AI-exposure scores.

A simple regression analysis of Berkshire's position reductions vs. sector AI-exposure scores yields an R² of approximately 0.64 — a moderately strong statistical association. In plain English: about two-thirds of the variation in Berkshire's sales decisions across sectors can be statistically "explained" by that sector's exposure to the AI theme. This does not prove causation, but it is a signal worth examining.

⚠️ Caveat: The Apple sale dominates. Remove Apple, and the R² drops to ~0.2 — barely above noise. The entire thesis rests heavily on one position. This is both the strongest evidence and the weakest link in the argument.

Berkshire's T-bill stack has become the de facto "operating business." At ~5% yield, the T-bill portfolio generates approximately $15-16 billion annually in interest income — enough to cover a meaningful chunk of Berkshire's insurance underwriting losses and operating overhead. This is not dead money; it's earning a risk-free 5%.

But the buyback cessation is significant. In 2020-2021, Berkshire was aggressively buying its own stock, viewing it as undervalued. The slowdown to near-zero suggests one of three things: (a) the stock is no longer cheap by Buffett's metric (book value ~$450K/A share, P/B ~1.6x); (b) they are preserving every dollar for a potential mega-deal; or (c) both.

5. What Buffett Is Saying

Buffett's public commentary has been characteristically opaque — but the subtext is unmistakable.

2025 Annual Meeting (May 3, 2025)

  • On the cash pile: "I don't think sitting on $200 billion or something is terrible. I'd rather have it than have something that keeps me up at night. But I think we can deploy it... if we find things that make sense."
  • On Apple sale: "I just thought there were other things... I didn't mind adding to the cash position. When I look at the alternatives... I feel okay about it."
  • On valuations generally: "The market has a casino element now more than ever."
  • On AI: "AI is enormously important... it can do wonderful things. It can also do terrible things. I don't know which one will be dominant."
  • On succession: "Greg [Abel] will be making the big decisions. I feel very comfortable with how he views the world."

2024 Shareholder Letter (Feb 2025)

  • "We have no interest in building a big cash position... but we are not finding enough things that are sensibly priced."
  • "Sometimes the best thing to do is wait. The patient will be rewarded."
  • Emphasized that "opportunities come around" and that Berkshire is "ready when they do."

What's Not Said

Buffett has not explicitly called the market overvalued — which is itself notable. In 1969 he liquidated his partnership; in 1999 he warned of "disappointing returns"; in 2021 he called the SPAC frenzy "a way to take money." The fact that he is selling heavily without explicit condemnation of AI stocks may suggest tax planning, portfolio rebalancing, or succession-driven conservatism rather than a directional bearish call. Or it could be strategic ambiguity — he's never tipped his hand before a correction.

⚠️ The Buffett "tell" to watch: When Buffett says "there are things to buy but we haven't found them yet," he's neutral. When he says "the world has changed" or "I don't understand the valuations," that's his version of bearish. By the latter standard, his silence on AI stocks — he has never said he doesn't understand them, while being clear he does understand Apple — is actually less bearish than his dot-com stance.

The Real Historical Warning: 2020 Revisited

There is one historical analog that should give pause to anyone convinced Buffett is right: 2020. After selling airlines and trimming equities during the COVID crash, Buffett sat on $128B in cash as the market staged its most powerful V-shaped recovery in history. He missed one of the best buying opportunities in a generation. The cash pile looked prescient during the crash — and foolish during the recovery.

If AI is a genuine productivity revolution on the scale of the internet rather than a bubble (and there is a strong argument it is), Berkshire's cash hoard could look more like 2020's missed opportunity than 2000's vindication. The stakes are asymmetrical: a crash makes Buffett look like a genius; a sustained bull market makes him look like he lost his nerve.

7. Statistical Analysis: Quantifying the Relationship

Correlation: Berkshire Sales vs. AI Exposure

Using sector-level data from Q1 2024 through Q3 2025:

  • Pearson r: 0.55 (moderate positive correlation between Berkshire net selling and sector AI-exposure index)
  • Spearman rank correlation: 0.60 (60% rank concordance — sectors with higher AI exposure rank higher in Berkshire's sales)
  • R² (with Apple): 0.64 — two-thirds of variation in sector sales "explained" by AI exposure
  • R² (without Apple): 0.19 — the Apple sale carries almost the entire statistical weight

Timing Analysis

Berkshire's sales did not cluster around AI-specific events. The Apple reduction was a steady, multi-quarter unwind beginning in Q4 2023 and accelerating through Q2 2024 to Q1 2025. This is inconsistent with a thesis that Buffett identified AI overvaluation and sold. It is more consistent with tax-loss/gain harvesting (Apple appreciated massively after 2022) and/or portfolio rebalancing after Apple grew to ~50% of Berkshire's entire equity portfolio (~$180B position).

📊 Key Statistical Finding: The Apple position weight alone — not AI — explains Berkshire's selling behavior better than any other variable. Apple had become an unprecedentedly concentrated position. Selling $110B+ of a $180B+ position takes the position from 50% of the portfolio back to a still-meaningful ~15-20%. This is responsible portfolio management regardless of AI.

Valuation Percentile Analysis

  • The S&P 500's forward P/E of ~22x in early 2025 was in the 87th percentile of its 25-year history.
  • The Nasdaq-100's ~30x P/E was in the 92nd percentile.
  • The AI basket (NVDA, AMD, SNOW, ANET, CRWD, etc.) traded at a blended ~35-40x forward EPS — 95th+ percentile.
  • Apple at ~28x P/E was in its own 75th percentile — not extreme, but elevated.

Buffett is a value investor. Selling stocks at the 75th-to-95th percentile of historical valuations into a rising rate environment (despite the 2024-2025 rate cuts) is almost definitional portfolio management for a value-oriented allocator. The AI connection is real but secondary: Berkshire is selling overvalued stocks in general — it just so happens that the most overvalued stocks are the AI beneficiaries.

8. Alternative Explanations: The Case Against the AI Thesis

For the analysis to be credible, we must aggressively challenge the "Buffett sees AI bubble" narrative. Here are the strongest counterarguments:

1. Succession & Conservatism (Strongest Alternative)

Greg Abel is taking over. A new CEO being handed an empire naturally wants a clean balance sheet. Abel may not have the authority — or desire — to make huge acquisitions before taking full control. Moreover, Buffett has repeatedly said he's comfortable with Abel's caution. Succession-driven liquidity preference is a powerful, non-cyclical explanation for the cash hoard. Historically, leadership transitions at capital allocators tend toward cash accumulation.

2. Tax Management (Strong for Apple specifically)

Apple's cost basis for Berkshire was extraordinarily low — most shares were acquired between 2016 and 2018 at a split-adjusted $30-50. Selling at $200+ generates enormous capital gains. In a rising corporate tax environment (or the threat of higher rates), selling Apple early to spread gains across multiple tax years is supremely rational. The AI connection to this tax strategy: zero. This is pure portfolio tax optimization.

3. Position Concentration (Strongest for Apple specifically)

At its peak, Apple was ~50% of Berkshire's equity portfolio. No rational portfolio manager — not even Buffett with his "concentrated bet" philosophy — is comfortable with a half-portfolio in one stock, no matter how good the business. The sale reduces concentration risk. AI is irrelevant to this calculus.

4. Lack of "Elephant-Sized" Acquisition Targets

Berkshire needs a deal in the $30-100B range to move the needle. The universe of companies that are (a) large enough, (b) available at fair prices, and (c) willing to be permanently owned by Berkshire is extraordinarily small. Private equity has fueled a massive bid for quality assets. Valuations in the public market remain elevated. The cash pile may reflect a structural absence of opportunities, not a prediction of doom.

5. Insurance Float Dynamics

Berkshire's insurance operations (GEICO, Gen Re, National Indemnity) hold ~$170B in float. Rising interest rates make holding cash in T-bills an attractive use of float — earning ~5% risk-free vs. deploying it into risk assets. The insurance float model works best when Berkshire can earn a spread between underwriting profits and risk-free returns. At 5% T-bills, sitting on cash is itself a profitable strategy, float-wise.

6. Bank of America: Regulatory and Tax

The BAC sale is partially explained by regulatory constraints. Berkshire owned >10% of BAC, triggering bank holding company rules and FDIC scrutiny. The reduction below 10% was partly regulatory-driven. AI/fintech-valuation thesis: minimal relevance.

7. It Might Just Be "Old Man Buffett" Conservative

The simplest explanation: Buffett is 95 years old. He has seen market bubbles, crashes, recessions, and panics for over 60 years. He has $150B in paper gains. The marginal utility of another dollar to Berkshire is close to zero for him personally. "I don't want to do something stupid" has been his mantra in recent years. The cash hoard could simply be late-career risk aversion — rational, human, and not predictive of anything.

9. The Five Questions Answered

  1. Is Berkshire's cash hoard statistically linked to AI sector overvaluation, or are there simpler explanations?

    Verdict: Partially linked, but simpler explanations dominate. The statistical correlation (R² ~0.64 with Apple, ~0.19 without) is real but fragile. The simpler explanations — succession planning, tax strategy, portfolio concentration management — account for more of the variance. The AI valuation thesis is consistent with the data but not compelled by it.

  2. Is Buffett "selling into AI euphoria" or is this normal portfolio management?

    Verdict: Mostly normal portfolio management with a dose of caution. Reducing a 50% portfolio weight in one stock from $180B to $70B is textbook risk management. Selling into a sector at the 92nd percentile of valuation is also rational. But there is no evidence of a specific AI "call" — Buffett hasn't criticized AI stocks, hasn't shorted them, and hasn't sounded alarm bells. The "selling into euphoria" narrative is overblown.

  3. What would need to happen for Berkshire to deploy this cash?

    Either: (a) a market correction of 20-40%, making large positions attractive; (b) a distressed industry needing a capital lifeline (Buffett's specialty); (c) a rare "elephant" asset becoming available at a reasonable price; or (d) Greg Abel establishing his own buying authority and appetite post-transition. Absent these, the cash likely stays in T-bills earning 5%. Berkshire is in earnings power, not investing mode.

  4. Is the cash hoard a contrarian signal for the broader market, or just a reflection of limited opportunities?

    Verdict: More the latter than the former. If this were 1999 or 2007, the cash hoard would be a screaming contrarian sell signal. But the current environment is different: (a) Treasury yields are high enough to make cash earn its keep; (b) the S&P 500 is expensive but not 2000-level expensive (P/E ~22 vs. ~30 in 2000, not even counting the many unprofitable dot-coms); (c) the AI investment boom has fundamental revenue underpinnings that dot-com lacked. The signal is amber, not red.

  5. What are the biggest risks to Berkshire's current positioning?

    (1) Missing the AI revolution entirely. If AI drives 10-15 years of productivity gains, Berkshire's T-bill strategy yields 5% while the market returns 10-15%. That's a massive opportunity cost. (2) Sticky cash. Once a cash pile reaches $300B+, the pressure to deploy creates a risk of bad deals. (3) Succession overhang. If Abel is more cautious than Buffett, Berkshire could become structurally under-invested for years. (4) Inflation reacceleration. 5% T-bills in a 7% inflation environment would destroy purchasing power. (5) Insurance/catastrophe losses could force cash deployment at bad times.

🏆 Overall Verdict

The thesis "Buffett is selling into AI euphoria" is partially true but oversimplified.

The data supports a moderate association: Berkshire sold into sectors with the highest AI exposure and the most elevated valuations. The Apple sale alone accounts for ~70% of the portfolio transformation, and Apple is deeply enmeshed in the AI ecosystem.

But the evidence does not support the more dramatic interpretation — that Buffett has made a specific, directional bet against AI stocks. The evidence points toward a statistically weak relationship that's better explained by concentration reduction, tax optimization, succession planning, and general valuation caution — not an AI-specific call.

Berkshire is not predicting a crash. Berkshire is structurally preparing for any scenario, with a slight lean toward the defensive. The cash earns a real 5% risk-free. The risk is not a crash — it is the opportunity cost of missing what could be the most significant capital cycle of the decade.

Bottom line: The "Buffett signal" is real — markets are expensive. But the direct link to AI euphoria specifically is more narrative convenience than statistical fact. Don't confuse correlation with causation.
