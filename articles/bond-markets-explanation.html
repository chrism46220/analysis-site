Bond Markets Explained

June 6, 2026

Why Bonds Matter (Even If You Only Trade Stocks)

If you've been investing strictly in equities, you've been operating in the high-octane lane. Bonds are the other engine — quieter, more complex, and arguably more important for understanding where the entire financial system is headed. The bond market is roughly twice the size of the global stock market (~$140 trillion vs ~$110 trillion). When something breaks, it usually breaks in bonds first.

This article covers what bonds are, the major types, how they trade, and what the current (June 2026) bond market is telling us about where the economy is going.

1. What a Bond Actually Is

A bond is a loan that trades. When you buy a bond, you lend money to the issuer (government, corporation, municipality) in exchange for periodic interest payments and the return of your principal at a fixed future date.

Key vocabulary:

  • Face value (par): The amount the issuer pays you at maturity. Almost always $1,000 per bond. When a bond trades above $1,000, it's "premium." Below $1,000, it's "discount."
  • Coupon rate: The annual interest rate the bond pays, expressed as a percentage of face value. A 5% coupon on a $1,000 bond pays $50/year, typically in two $25 semi-annual payments.
  • Maturity date: The date the issuer repays face value. Could be 4 weeks (T-bill) or 30 years (long bond).
  • Yield to maturity (YTM): The total return you'll earn if you hold the bond to maturity, accounting for coupon payments and any difference between the purchase price and face value. This is the number to compare across bonds.
  • Credit rating: Assessment of the issuer's ability to repay. Investment grade (AAA to BBB-/Baa3) vs high yield / "junk" (BB+/Ba1 and below). Issued by Moody's, S&P, and Fitch.

The Golden Rule: Prices Move Inverse to Yields

This is the single most important concept in bond markets. A $1,000 bond paying $50/year has a 5% yield. If interest rates rise and new bonds pay 6%, nobody will pay $1,000 for your 5% bond. Its price falls until the $50 coupon equals a 6% yield — that's roughly $833. Conversely, if rates fall to 4%, your 5% bond becomes more valuable and trades above par.

Price Down = Yields Up. Price Up = Yields Down. Memorize this.

Duration — Measuring Interest Rate Sensitivity

Duration (measured in years) tells you roughly how much a bond's price will change for a 1% move in interest rates. A bond with a duration of 5 years will fall roughly 5% if rates rise 1%. Longer maturities = higher duration = more price volatility. This is why 30-year Treasuries can swing 15–20% in a year despite being "safe" — the price risk is significant even when default risk is zero.

2. Types of Bonds

Treasuries

Issued by the U.S. government. Considered risk-free because the U.S. can always print money to repay. The benchmark for all other bonds.

  • Treasury Bills (T-Bills): Maturities of 4, 8, 13, 17, 26, and 52 weeks. No coupon — sold at a discount and redeemed at face value. The difference is your interest.
  • Treasury Notes: 2, 3, 5, 7, and 10-year maturities. Pay semi-annual coupons. The 10-year note is the most important single security in global finance — the "risk-free" benchmark for everything: mortgages, corporate bonds, sovereign debt pricing.
  • Treasury Bonds: 20 and 30-year maturities. Higher duration, more price volatility.
  • TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities): Principal adjusts with CPI. If inflation runs at 3%, a $1,000 TIPS becomes $1,030 in principal, and the coupon (say 1.5%) pays on the inflation-adjusted amount. The "real yield" is the nominal yield minus expected inflation. Currently (June 2026) 10-year real yields are around 1.8–2.0%, well above the near-zero levels of 2020–2022.
  • STRIPS: Treasury securities that have had their coupon payments stripped into individual zero-coupon bonds. Each coupon payment and the final principal become separate securities trading at a discount.

Agency Bonds

Issued by government-sponsored enterprises like Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Not explicitly guaranteed by the U.S. government (unlike Treasuries) but widely viewed as having implicit backing. Yields are slightly higher than Treasuries for the same maturity — 15–40 basis points (0.15–0.40%) more.

Municipal Bonds ("Munis")

Issued by states, cities, counties, and special-purpose districts. Two types:

  • General Obligation (GO) bonds: Backed by the issuer's full taxing authority. These are the safest munis — the government entity pledges to raise taxes if needed to repay.
  • Revenue bonds: Backed by the revenue from a specific project (tolls from a bridge, fees from a water utility, ticket sales from a stadium). Higher risk than GO bonds because they depend on project success.

The key attraction: municipal bond interest is federal tax-free and often state tax-free if you live in the issuing state. For someone in the 32% federal bracket, a 3.5% muni yield is equivalent to a 5.15% taxable yield.

Corporate Bonds

Two broad tiers:

  • Investment Grade (IG): Rated BBB-/Baa3 or higher. Think Microsoft, Johnson & Johnson, Apple. Default rates historically below 0.5% over 5-year horizons. Currently yielding ~4.6–5.2% for 7–10 year maturities (as of June 2026).
  • High Yield (HY) / "Junk": Rated BB+/Ba1 and below. Think distressed companies, leveraged buyouts, smaller issuers. Default rates historically 2–5% in normal years, spiking to 8–12% in recessions. Currently yielding 6.5–8.5%.

The spread — the difference between corporate yields and Treasury yields of the same maturity — is the market's measure of credit risk. The Bloomberg IG OAS (Option-Adjusted Spread) was ~110–120 bps in May 2026, slightly above the 10-year average of ~100 bps, suggesting modest caution but no panic.

Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS) & Asset-Backed Securities (ABS)

MBS are pools of residential mortgages packaged into bonds. The biggest MBS issuer is Ginnie Mae (government guaranteed) and Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac (agency). Investors get the cash flow from mortgage payments — principal and interest.

ABS are similar but backed by other assets: auto loans, credit card receivables, student loans, equipment leases. They're structured in tranches — senior tranches get paid first (lower yield, higher rating), junior tranches absorb losses first (higher yield, lower rating).

MBS have a unique risk called prepayment risk: when rates fall, homeowners refinance and pay off old mortgages early, forcing investors to reinvest at lower rates.

International & Sovereign Bonds

Debt issued by foreign governments. Vastly different risk profiles:

  • Developed sovereigns: German Bunds (yielding ~2.5%), UK Gilts (~4.2%), Japanese JGBs (~0.8–1.0%). Safe but low yields.
  • Emerging market sovereigns: Yield 5–12% depending on credit quality. Brazil, Indonesia, Mexico, South Africa. Currency risk is real — even if the bond performs, a depreciating local currency can destroy USD returns.

Green Bonds

Bonds whose proceeds are earmarked for environmental projects (renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transportation). Issuers include sovereigns (the EU is the largest), corporations (Apple, NextEra), and agencies. The market has grown from near-zero in 2013 to over $1 trillion annually by 2025. Yields are typically 1–5 bps lower than conventional equivalents (the "greenium") — investors accept slightly less return for the ESG label.

3. How Bonds Are Traded

Unlike stocks, bonds are a dealer market, not an exchange market. This is the single biggest practical difference for an equity trader learning fixed income.

Primary Market — Where New Bonds Are Born

Treasuries: The U.S. Treasury holds regular auctions through the New York Fed. Competitive bidders (primary dealers — the big banks) set yields. Non-competitive bidders (individuals via TreasuryDirect) accept the auction-clearing yield. Auctions are weekly for T-bills, monthly for most notes/bonds, quarterly for 20 and 30-year bonds.

Corporates/Munis: An underwriter (Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley) buys the entire new issue from the company and sells it to institutional investors. This is called an offering. The underwriter sets the coupon and initial price, and the bond starts trading in the secondary market the day after pricing.

Secondary Market — Where Existing Bonds Trade

There is no NYSE or NASDAQ equivalent for bonds. Trading happens through a decentralized network of dealers:

  • An investor calls a dealer and asks for a quote on a specific bond.
  • The dealer shows a bid (what they'll pay to buy) and an ask (what they'll sell for).
  • The difference is the bid-ask spread — the dealer's compensation for providing liquidity.
  • The trade is negotiated bilaterally and then reported to a trade reporting system (TRACE for corporates, MSRB for munis).

Why Bond Liquidity Is Worse Than Stock Liquidity

  • Fragmentation: There are millions of individual CUSIPs (bond IDs) — each with different coupons, maturities, and issuance dates. Apple stock is one ticker. Apple bonds: dozens. This thins out trading volume across every individual issue.
  • Dealer balance sheets are smaller: Post-2008 regulations (Volcker Rule, Basel III) reduced banks' ability to hold bond inventories. Dealers used to warehouse bonds and provide deep liquidity. Now they act more as matchmakers.
  • Corporate bonds are "buy and hold": A large portion sits in insurance company and pension fund portfolios that rarely trade. Turnover is a fraction of equity turnover.

Electronic trading has improved liquidity significantly (MarketAxess, Tradeweb handle ~30% of corporate bond volume), but it's still not click-and-trade like equities. Large institutional trades often require negotiating with multiple dealers to find the best price.

Settlement

Most bonds settle T+1 (one business day after trade) as of mid-2025. Some international bonds settle T+2. Treasuries settle T+1. Compare to stocks (also T+1 now, after shifting from T+2 in May 2024).

A Useful Analogy

Think of bonds less like the stock market (one exchange, tight spreads, instant liquidity) and more like the car market (thousands of unique vehicles, prices that depend on negotiating with a dealer, and meaningful transaction costs if you want to sell quickly).

The Yield Curve Is No Longer Inverted — That's the Signal

From mid-2022 through mid-2025, the yield curve was inverted (short-term yields higher than long-term yields). This is the most reliable recession indicator in finance — every U.S. recession since 1955 has been preceded by an inverted curve. But here's the critical detail: recessions tend to begin not when the curve inverts, but when it un-inverts.

The mechanics: When the Fed cuts rates in response to a slowing economy, short-term yields fall. Long-term yields don't fall as much because inflation remains sticky. The curve steepens. The un-inversion is confirmation that the economic deceleration the market feared is actually arriving.

We are in that window right now. The curve uninverted in late 2025, and a mild recession (1–2 quarters of negative GDP) is now the base case for Q3–Q4 2026. The bond market's forecasting track record on recessions is far better than that of equity markets or economists' surveys — because bond traders put real money on the line with clear directional conviction.

Why Short-to-Intermediate Duration (2–7 Years) Makes Sense

Given the outlook of a mild recession within 6 months, the bond market strategy favored in the June 2026 Investment Strategy focuses on the 2–7 year part of the curve. Here's why:

  • Cash (T-bills, money market): Yields are still decent (~3.5–3.8%), but the Fed is expected to cut 50–100 bps in the next 6–9 months. You lock in today's rate for a few weeks, then reinvest at a lower rate. You miss the price appreciation that longer-duration bonds will experience as rates fall.
  • Short-intermediate bonds (2–7yr): These capture significant price appreciation as the Fed cuts. If the 5-year yield drops from ~3.9% to 3.2% over 12 months, a 5-year bond with a ~4.5-year duration gains roughly 3.1% in price — on top of the ~3.9% coupon yield. That's a 7% annualized return in the "safe" part of the portfolio.
  • Long bonds (20–30yr): Higher duration means higher upside if rates fall — a 30-year could gain 15–20% in a cutting cycle. But the risk is asymmetric: if inflation reaccelerates (the stagflation scenario), long bonds could lose 20–30%. In a mild recession with sticky inflation, the long end is a gamble, not a hedge.

The 2–7 year part of the curve offers the best risk/reward: meaningful upside from rate cuts, limited duration risk if reacceleration happens, and yields that still beat inflation by ~0.5–1.0% today.

Why TIPS for CPI Protection

CPI at 3.29% is above the Fed's 2% target and showing signs of reacceleration (tariff passthrough, rent stickiness). Allocating part of the bond sleeve to TIPS provides:

  • Direct inflation pass-through: If CPI runs 3.3%, a 5-year TIPS with a 1.9% real yield returns 1.9% + 3.3% = 5.2% nominal. If CPI accelerates to 4%, the TIPS return rises to 5.9%. A nominal Treasury's 4.3% yield stays flat.
  • Current real yields are attractive: 1.8–2.0% real yields for TIPS are the highest since the 2008 financial crisis. From 2010–2021, TIPS real yields were frequently negative or near-zero. You're being paid historically generous real returns for inflation protection.

The 5-year TIPS breakeven inflation rate (the difference between nominal and TIPS yields) is currently ~2.4%, implying the market expects inflation to average 2.4% over the next 5 years. If you believe inflation will be higher — as the mild recession / sticky inflation scenario suggests — TIPS are cheap.

Why Investment Grade Corporates, Not High Yield

With a recession expected, why buy any corporate bonds at all?

  • IG credit spreads are moderate, not tight: At ~115 bps, IG spreads are slightly above the 10-year average. You're being fairly compensated for default risk. The mild recession scenario implies a default rate of 1–2% for IG — losses that are more than covered by the spread pickup over Treasuries.
  • High yield is a different story: HY spreads at ~370 bps are near the low end of the historical range. In a recession, HY default rates could hit 5–8%. The spread isn't big enough to compensate. HY bonds also tend to behave like equities during downturns — correlation spikes, which defeats the purpose of holding bonds as a portfolio hedge.
  • Duration + credit = double leverage: For IG, you want to lean into the duration trade (betting on rate cuts) while collecting a modest credit spread. For HY, the credit risk overwhelms the duration benefit — the bond might fall even as rates decline, because the market worries about defaults.

Risks to This Positioning

Every bond strategy has failure modes. Here are the three to watch:

1. Reacceleration (no recession, CPI pops back to 4%+)

If tariffs drive renewed inflation and the consumer holds up better than expected, the Fed cannot cut. The mild recession is postponed. Bond yields rise, and intermediate-duration bonds lose 3–5% in price. TIPS outperform nominal bonds in this scenario. The portfolio would be dragged down by rate-sensitive equity sectors (growth, real estate) as 10-year yields approach 5%.

2. Stagflation (recession + CPI stays at 3.5–4%)

Growth stalls, but inflation stays elevated. The Fed is pinned — can't cut, can't tighten. This is the worst environment for long bonds (little rate relief, and real purchasing power is eroded). Short-duration bonds and TIPS excel here: TIPS give direct inflation pass-through, short bonds roll quickly into higher coupons, and cash earns a decent nominal yield. IG corporates suffer from widening credit spreads as recession bites, but at least the coupon is solid. This scenario reinforces the strategy that already avoids long nominal bonds.

3. Deep recession (GDP −3% or worse)

A credit event or external shock triggers a severe downturn. The Fed cuts 200+ bps. Long bonds are the best asset class — 30-year Treasuries could return 30%+. The 2–7 year strategy would still work well (7–10% returns), just not maximally. The risk is concentrated in HY bonds, which could see 10%+ default rates and 15–20% principal losses. This is why avoiding HY is critical.

5. Putting It All Together

If you're coming from equities, the bond market can feel like a foreign language — inverted curves, OAS, real yields, duration. But the core logic is straightforward:

  • Bonds are the price discovery mechanism for the time value of money (interest rates) and credit risk (the probability of not getting paid back).
  • The bond market has correctly called every recession in modern history, often 6–18 months in advance.
  • In June 2026, the curve is uninverted, CPI is sticky at 3.29%, and a mild recession is the base case. The right bond response is: short-to-intermediate duration (2–7yr), a TIPS allocation for inflation protection, investment grade corporates for modest credit pick-up, and a hard pass on high yield and long nominal Treasuries.

This isn't the bond market of 2020 (zero rates, buy everything) or 2022 (rates surging, sell everything). It's a cautious middle ground — harvesting respectable yields while positioning for the rate cuts that a slowing economy will demand. As always, the bond market is telling us what's coming. The question is whether we're listening.
