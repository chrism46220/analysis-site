Quantum Computing Through the Lens of Historical Technology Analogs

April 28, 2026

Quantum Computing Through the Lens of Historical Technology Analogs

Date: April 28, 2026 Purpose: Identify where quantum computing sits in its development cycle by comparing it to transformative technologies of the past.

Executive Summary

Quantum computing in early 2026 most closely resembles artificial intelligence in the mid-to-late 1990s — technically demonstrable, commercially nascent, generating real (but modest) revenue from early adopters, while the foundational science advances rapidly toward a potential breakout. The first fault-tolerant logical qubit demonstrations (Google's Willow, IonQ's Walking Cat Architecture) parallel the period just before AI's AlexNet moment: the underlying theory is proven, the scaling principles are understood, but the engineering to deliver a step-change in capability is still 1–3 years away. Like the internet in 1992 (pre-Mosaic), like semiconductors in 1955 (pre-integrated circuit), and like the Human Genome Project in 1998 (working draft imminent), quantum is past the "pure research" phase but has not yet reached the "commercially transformative" phase. The critical question is not whether fault-tolerant quantum computing arrives, but when — a timeline that depends on error correction scaling, physical qubit fidelity improvements, and the discovery of genuinely useful quantum advantage.

Step 1: The Historical Analogs

1.1 Semiconductors (1947 → 1971 → Everything)

Early-stage characteristics (1947–1960): - The point-contact transistor (1947) was a lab curiosity. It cost ~$20 in 1947 dollars (~$270 today) per unit — far more expensive than the vacuum tubes it would eventually replace. Yield rates were abysmal; early transistors were unreliable, temperature-sensitive, and difficult to manufacture consistently. - Adoption was driven entirely by niche military and aerospace applications that could accept high costs. The first commercial application, the transistor radio (Regency TR-1, 1954), was a novelty: it cost $49.95 (~$570 today) when the average weekly wage was ~$75. Only 100,000 units sold in its first year. - Key barrier: manufacturing consistency. No two transistors from the same batch behaved identically. There was no "foundry" model — each company fabricated its own devices using bespoke processes.

Key inflection points: - 1958–59: Jack Kilby's integrated circuit and Robert Noyce's planar process solved the interconnection problem. Instead of wiring transistors by hand, you etched them onto a single piece of silicon. This was the moment semiconductor economics flipped: complexity became nearly free. - 1965: Gordon Moore observed the doubling trend. Moore's Law became a self-fulfilling industry roadmap, aligning R&D investment across dozens of companies. - 1971: Intel 4004 microprocessor — the first time an entire CPU fit on one chip. This created the personal computing market. Intel had ~$9.4M in revenue in 1970; by 1980 it was $855M.

Time from promising to transformative: The transistor was invented in 1947. The first viable microprocessor shipped in 1971. That's roughly 24 years. However, the exponential decay in cost-per-transistor meant that the rate of transformation accelerated once the economics flipped. Semiconductor industry revenue grew from ~$445M in 1959 to $14.2B by 1979 — a 32x increase over 20 years.

What it teaches about quantum: The key variable was manufacturing yield and consistency, not physics. Once semiconductors became reproducible (planar process, IC), the scaling trajectory became predictable. For quantum, the analog is error correction — the ability to build logical qubits that consistently outperform physical ones is the "planar process" moment.

1.2 Classical Computing (1945 → 1981 → Mainstream)

Early-stage characteristics (1945–1960): - ENIAC (1945) cost ~$487,000 (~$8M today), filled 2,000 square feet, consumed 200 kilowatts, and ran on ~18,000 vacuum tubes that failed every few minutes. Programming required re-cabling a patch panel. There was exactly one customer (the U.S. Army). - By 1955, there were only ~250 computers in the United States. IBM sold or leased about 20 mainframes per year. Every machine was custom-built; there was no software compatibility between models. - Key barrier: cost and reliability. Vacuum tube failure rates meant uptime was measured in hours. Transistorized computers (TRADIC, 1954; IBM 608, 1957) improved dramatically but remained room-sized.

Key inflection points: - 1964: IBM System/360 — the first compatible family of computers, from small commercial to large scientific. You could write code once and run it on any model. This was the "platform" moment: it created a software ecosystem and decoupled hardware procurement from application development. - 1970s: Minicomputers (DEC PDP-11, VAX) brought computing to departmental and lab budgets — ~$20K–$100K instead of $1M+. Single-user interactive computing replaced batch processing. - 1981: IBM PC — the personal computer for the office. $1,565 base price (~$5,500 today). By 1985, PC sales exceeded mainframe sales for the first time.

Time from promising to transformative: ENIAC (1945) to IBM PC (1981) is 36 years. But the System/360 (1964) was arguably the moment computing became a true industry. IBM's mainframe revenue grew from $3.6B in 1965 to $25B by 1985. The PC compressed the timeline further — from 1981 to 1995, the installed base went from near-zero to ~200 million units.

What it teaches about quantum: The System/360 lesson is the most relevant: compatibility and standardization were more important than raw performance. Quantum computing today has no System/360 moment — there is no dominant architecture, no standard instruction set, no compatible software layer across trapped-ion, superconducting, photonic, and neutral-atom approaches. The industry is still in its "each machine is custom" phase.

1.3 The Internet (1969 → 1993 → 2000)

Early-stage characteristics (1969–1990): - ARPANET (1969) connected four university research computers. By 1983, there were ~500 hosts. All traffic was non-commercial by policy (NSFNET Acceptable Use Policy expressly forbade "commercial activity" until 1991). - Early internet services were text-based, required command-line expertise, and offered no graphical interface. Email was the "killer app" for academics, but there were no consumer applications. - Key barrier: the user interface. The internet had extraordinary potential, but no one could access it without technical training. Content was sparse — there were essentially no commercial websites until 1993.

Key inflection points: - 1983: TCP/IP became the universal standard, enabling heterogeneous networks to interoperate. This was the "networking" analog of the planar process: it made interconnection predictable. - 1989–91: Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web (HTML, HTTP, URLs) at CERN. The web was released to the public in 1991 — but still text-only. - 1993: NCSA Mosaic — the first graphical web browser that displayed images inline. This was the breakthrough. In 1993, the web grew 341,634% in traffic volume. By 1994, there were 3,000 websites. By 1997, 1 million. - 1994–95: Netscape Navigator IPO (Aug 1995) valued the company at $2.9B on $16.6M revenue — 174x trailing sales. The dot-com bubble followed.

Time from promising to transformative: ARPANET (1969) to Mosaic (1993) is 24 years. But the transformation was compressible — the web went from 50 sites to millions in 4 years. The internet waited 24 years for a usable interface; once it arrived, adoption exploded.

What it teaches about quantum: The killer variable was accessibility — Mosaic didn't change the underlying network, but it made the network usable. Quantum computing's "Mosaic moment" will be a fault-tolerant quantum cloud service that any programmer can call via API, without understanding error correction, decoherence, or qubit topology. Today's quantum cloud services (Amazon Braket, Azure Quantum, Google Quantum AI) are more like ARPANET in 1983: functional for experts, impenetrable for everyone else.

1.4 Artificial Intelligence (1956 → 1997 → 2012)

Early-stage characteristics (1956–1990s): - The Dartmouth Conference (1956) coined "artificial intelligence" with immense optimism. By the 1970s, this optimism collided with reality: computing power was insufficient, algorithms didn't scale, and the field entered the first "AI winter" (1974–1980). - Expert systems (R1/XCON at DEC, 1980) achieved real commercial success — XCON saved DEC an estimated $40M/year. But they were brittle, required manual knowledge engineering, and couldn't learn. - The second AI winter (1987–1993) saw the collapse of the LISP machine market and the end of Japan's Fifth Generation project. Total AI venture funding dropped to near zero. - By the mid-1990s, AI was perceived as a failed field. The term "AI" was actively avoided in commercial contexts.

Key inflection points: - 1997: IBM Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov at chess. This was a symbolic breakthrough — it proved that a narrow AI system could outperform the best human at a defined task. But Deep Blue was specialized (brute-force search + custom hardware), not generalizable. - 2000s: The statistical revolution. Machine learning shifted from rule-based systems to statistical pattern recognition. Support vector machines, Bayesian networks, and ensemble methods achieved practical results in specific domains (spam filtering, credit scoring, recommendation systems). Google's PageRank (1998) was an ML algorithm at heart. Speech recognition (Dragon NaturallySpeaking, 1997) reached commercial viability. - 2004–2006: Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and others revived deep neural networks. Key insight: training deep networks was feasible with GPUs and big datasets, despite earlier belief that backpropagation didn't work for deep architectures. - 2012: Alex Krizhevsky's AlexNet won the ImageNet competition with 15.3% error rate (second place was 26.2%). This was the breakthrough moment. The error rate was so dramatically better that it triggered a wholesale shift of the entire computer vision field to deep learning. Within 3 years, error rates dropped below human performance.

Time from promising to transformative: Dartmouth (1956) to AlexNet (2012) is 56 years — by far the longest gestation. But the effective timeline from the symbolic inflection point (Deep Blue, 1997) to the deep learning explosion (2012) is 15 years, and from the algorithmic foundations (backpropagation + GPUs, 2006) to breakout is only 6 years. AI was "40 years away" for 40 years — and then arrived. The key lesson: scientific progress and commercial viability can diverge by decades.

What it teaches about quantum: The direct parallels are striking. Quantum computing has had its own "first winter" (1990s–2000s, when it was unclear whether any scalable quantum computer could be built). It is now in its "pre-2012 AI phase": clear scientific progress, demonstrable but fragile systems, growing investment but no breakthrough application. The "AlexNet moment" for quantum would be a practical demonstration of unconditional quantum advantage for a commercially valuable problem — not Shor's algorithm factoring 15 into 3×5, but a real problem that no classical computer can solve within a time or cost budget that matters.

1.5 Biotechnology / Human Genome Project (1990 → 2003 → 2015)

Early-stage characteristics (1990–1999): - The Human Genome Project launched in 1990 with a planned 15-year timeline and a $3B budget. Sequencing technology was primitive: Sanger sequencing produced ~500 base pairs per run. The first complete chromosome (chromosome 22, 1999) took 9 years. - Sequencing one human genome cost ~$300M in 1990. Only governments and large research consortia could afford it. The commercial sector was skeptical — Celera Genomics launched in 1998 promising to do it faster (3 years for $300M), but the business model was uncertain. - Key barrier: sequencing throughput and cost per base. The enabling technology wasn't the genome map itself, but the industrial-scale sequencing machines that made the map possible.

Key inflection points: - 2000–2003: Working draft (June 2000) and complete genome (April 2003). The project finished 2+ years early and under budget. This was the completion of the "map" — but not yet the era of precision medicine. - 2005–2010: Next-generation sequencing (Illumina Solexa, 454 Life Sciences) reduced cost per genome from $100M (2001) to ~$10,000 (2010) to ~$1,000 (2015). This was the true commercial inflection: a $1,000 genome changed the economics of healthcare. - 2012–2020: CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (2012), CAR-T cell therapy, mRNA vaccine platform (proven in 2020). The genome map plus cheap sequencing enabled an entire ecosystem of therapeutics.

Time from promising to transformative: HGP launch (1990) to the $1,000 genome (2015) is 25 years. But the $3B genome (1990) → $100M genome (2001) → $1M genome (2007) → $100K genome (2010) → $1K genome (2015) shows an exponential cost curve, not linear. Once sequencing cost dropped below ~$10K, the application space expanded dramatically.

What it teaches about quantum: The HGP analog is especially powerful: quantum computing is currently in the "sequencing a genome costs $300M" phase — technically feasible, but too expensive and unreliable for broad commercial deployment. The question is what "next-generation sequencing" will be for quantum: will qubit fidelity improvements deliver the equivalent of Illumina's price collapse? Or will quantum require dozens of separate breakthroughs (error correction, interconnects, algorithm development) that don't compress?

Step 2: Mapping Quantum Computing to These Analogs

Where Quantum Computing Is Today

Quantum computing in 2026 occupies a position roughly analogous to:

Analog Phase Year Equivalent
Semiconductors Post-transistor, pre-integrated circuit ~1954–1956
Classical Computing Post-mainframe, pre-minicomputer explosion ~1958–1964
Internet Post-TCP/IP, pre-Mosaic ~1991–1992
Artificial Intelligence Post-Deep Blue, pre-AlexNet ~1998–2004
Biotechnology Post-working-draft, pre-$1K genome ~2003–2008

The tightest analog is AI in the 1998–2004 period — the phase between the symbolic milestone (Deep Blue beating Kasparov) and the algorithmic/engineering breakthrough (AlexNet). The characteristics match:

Present (2026) characteristics: 1. Symbolic milestones have been achieved. Google Willow (Dec 2024) demonstrated "below threshold" error correction — errors decreased as qubit count increased. IonQ demonstrated 99.99% 2Q gate fidelity and published a detailed fault-tolerant architecture. These are real scientific achievements — like Deep Blue beating Kasparov — but they do not yet constitute practical quantum advantage for any commercially meaningful problem.

  1. Systems are noisy, fragile, and expensive. NISQ-era processors (50–1,000+ physical qubits) operate with error rates of 0.1–1% per gate operation. Error correction overhead is massive: current logical qubits require ~30–100 physical qubits each. A typical 50-qubit NISQ device costs $10M–$20M in hardware and requires a specialized team to operate. Cloud access fees are $5–$10 per circuit minute. This is the "mini-supercomputer" economics of the 1960s — only the well-funded can play.

  2. Revenue exists but is modest. IonQ ($130M FY2025) leads all pure-play quantum companies in revenue. D-Wave ($24.6M) has real annealing customers. Combined pure-play revenue is ~$162M — comparable to Netscape's $16.6M in 1994 (a company that IPO'd at $2.9B on that revenue). The quantum market (hardware + software + services) is estimated at ~$10B globally, but this includes R&D spending, government grants, consulting, and adjacent nanotechnology.

  3. No killer application has emerged. Despite years of NISQ-era exploration, no one has demonstrated a quantum algorithm running on real hardware that outperforms the best classical approach on a problem that any paying customer cares about. Quantum chemistry simulations and optimization problems remain at the "promising but unproven" stage. This is the most important gap — analogous to the web before Mosaic, or AI before AlexNet.

  4. Architectural diversity is high with no convergence. Trapped ions (IonQ, Quantinuum), superconducting loops (Google, IBM, Rigetti), photonic (PsiQuantum, Xanadu), neutral atoms (QuEra, Atom Computing), topological (Microsoft) — five+ distinct approaches, each with its own scaling challenges. This is the semiconductor industry of the 1950s, where silicon, germanium, and compound semiconductors competed without a clear winner. History suggests an architectural consolidation phase is coming, but it's unclear which approach will win.

Characteristics That Match Each Historical Phase

Matches semiconductors ~1954–1956: - Fundamental physics is understood (Shor 1994 = Shockley 1948) - Individual devices work but are unreliable and irreproducible at scale - Manufacturing is artisanal — each system is hand-tuned - Clear path to scaling (Moore's Law for transistors; error correction + qubit count for quantum) - Government/military funding dominates (DARPA, DOE, NSA)

Matches internet ~1991–1992: - The core protocols are standardizing (but quantum programming languages, compilers, and hardware abstractions are fragmented) - The underlying network exists (cloud quantum services) but user interfaces are poor - No graphic/web interface equivalent for quantum — using a quantum computer today requires understanding of quantum physics, gate-level operations, and noise mitigation - Traffic/usage grows exponentially from a tiny base

Matches biotech ~2003–2008: - The "map" (error-corrected quantum computing theory) is complete - The enabling technology for mass deployment (fault-tolerant logical qubits) is still too expensive and unreliable - Costs are declining but haven't hit the inflection point - Early applications (quantum chemistry, materials simulation) are clear in principle but not yet practical

Key Elements Still Missing

  1. A demonstrated quantum advantage on a commercially relevant problem. This is the single most important missing element. Without it, quantum computing is a promising technology without a product-market fit. The Shor's algorithm demonstration (factoring 15, 2001; factoring 21, 2012; factoring a 2,048-bit number? still infeasible) does not count.

  2. Reliable, large-scale error correction. Google's Willow and IonQ's roadmap show that the science of error correction works. The engineering of building a system with 1,000+ physical qubits that consistently operates below threshold is not yet proven at scale. The current record for logical qubits is ~4 (Quantinuum, 2025).

  3. Standardized software ecosystem. IBM's Qiskit is the closest to a dominant platform, but even Qiskit requires understanding of quantum circuit models. There is no "operating system" for quantum computing, no standard API, no high-level quantum language that abstracts hardware details. Every platform requires bespoke optimization for the specific qubit topology, gate set, and noise profile.

  4. Cost parity or advantage over classical alternatives. Current quantum computing costs are orders of magnitude higher than classical alternatives for any problem that can be solved classically. For quantum to matter, it must either solve problems that classical cannot (true quantum advantage) or solve problems much more efficiently (quantum speedup with acceptable overhead). Neither has been demonstrated at commercial scale.

  5. Talent and education. There are perhaps 3,000–5,000 people worldwide with deep expertise in both quantum information science and practical hardware. By comparison, ~300,000 people graduate annually with computer science degrees in the U.S. alone. The talent bottleneck constrains both R&D velocity and enterprise adoption.

Step 3: Leading Indicators of Transition

What Historical Signals Marked the Shift from Early-Stage to Breakout

Across all five analogs, the transition from "promising research" to "commercially transformative" was signaled by:

  1. A demonstrable, unambiguous superiority over the prior dominant approach. The integrated circuit made hand-wired circuits obsolete (cost/complexity). The IBM PC made terminals+minicomputer model obsolete (cost/accessibility). Mosaic made FTP+gopher+telnet obsolete (usability). AlexNet made RoBERTa-era CV algorithms obsolete (accuracy). CRISPR made earlier gene-editing approaches obsolete (precision).

  2. A dramatic, sustained cost decline. Semiconductors: transistor cost dropped from $20 (1947) to $0.01 (1970). Sequencing: $3B (1990) to $1,000 (2015). Computing: $8M (ENIAC, 1945) to $500 (PC, 1990). Deep learning: compute cost dropped 100x from 2010 to 2020 due to GPU scaling.

  3. Platform standardization. TCP/IP enabled the internet. IBM's System/360 architecture enabled enterprise computing. The x86 architecture + Windows enabled the PC revolution. Deep learning frameworks (TensorFlow, PyTorch) enabled the AI explosion. A single dominant approach or compatible set of approaches must emerge.

  4. A "killer application" that creates its own demand. Spreadsheets (VisiCalc, 1979) drove PC adoption. Email and the web drove internet adoption. Image classification and then LLMs drove AI adoption. Exome sequencing drove genomic test adoption. Each killer app was simple to understand, solved a real pain point, and had no adequate alternative.

  5. Investment crossing from government/R&D to mainstream VC/corporate budgets. The inflection point correlates with when practical application funding exceeds basic research funding. For AI, this happened around 2012–2014. For the internet, around 1994–1995. For semiconductors, around 1960. DARPA remains the dominant quantum funder; while VC and corporate investment has grown ($2.4B in 2023, per McKinsey), it still flows disproportionately to hard tech rather than application-layer companies.

The only "present" leading indicator is the rapid pace of scientific progress. Gate fidelities have improved from ~99% (2019) to ~99.99% (2025) for leading approaches. Error correction progress has been dramatic (Google Willow, Quantinuum logical qubits). Published photonic interconnects between trapped-ion systems (IonQ, 2026) suggest modular scaling is plausible. The science is advancing faster than the engineering.

What Would Change the Signal

The transition to breakout would be signaled by any of:

  1. A logical qubit demo with error <1e-7 on a 12+ logical qubit processor — IonQ has this on its 2026 roadmap. If achieved, it would be the quantum equivalent of Willow's "below threshold" scaled up to a useful level.

  2. A quantum chemistry result on real hardware that cannot be replicated classically — e.g., simulating a molecule of catalytic or pharmaceutical relevance where classical methods (DFT, coupled cluster) diverge in cost.

  3. A single company or consortium standardizing on one hardware platform — analogous to IBM winning the mainframe wars or x86 winning the PC wars. If IonQ, Quantinuum, or Google achieves a clear technology lead that pulls the ecosystem, standardization follows.

  4. Sequencing-cost-style collapse in error-corrected logical qubit cost — from $10M+/logical qubit today (rare earth) to something approaching the price of a classical HPC add-on (~$100K for a useful compute unit).

Step 4: Timeline Estimation

Scenario Framework

The timeline to commercially transformative quantum computing depends on three key variables:

  • Variable 1: Error correction scaling speed. How fast can the industry go from today's demonstration of 4 logical qubits (Quantinuum) to thousands of logical qubits with error rates <1e-10? This is the engineering equivalent of the planar process or the $1,000 genome.

  • Variable 2: Killer application discovery. Will a genuinely valuable problem appear that only quantum can solve, or will quantum need to compete on cost with rapidly improving classical alternatives (especially AI-accelerated classical simulation)?

  • Variable 3: Architectural consolidation. Does one qubit modality win (simplifying the ecosystem, enabling focused engineering), or does fragmentation persist (slowing software/hardware co-design)?

Key Dependencies

  • Error correction: The single highest-impact dependency. IonQ's Walking Cat Architecture and Google's Willow suggest the theory is sound. The question is whether scaling physical qubits while maintaining 99.99%+ fidelity across thousands of qubits is feasible within 5–7 years. Any fundamental unbounded noise source (environmental coupling, control crosstalk at scale) could delay timelines by 5–10 years.

  • Classical competition: Classical computing is not standing still. AI-driven classical methods (neural network wave functions, machine-learned force fields) are eroding the potential TAM for quantum in chemistry and materials simulation. If classical methods can extend their reach by another 5–10 years, the "quantum advantage" bar rises. This is the single greatest risk to quantum timeline estimates — the assumption that the classical frontier is static.

  • Talent and workforce: The shortage of quantum engineers, error correction specialists, and algorithm developers is a binding constraint on progress. Current university pipeline produces ~2,000–3,000 quantum-focused PhDs/year globally. Scaling this by 5–10x would require a decade.

  • Geopolitical investment: The U.S. (National Quantum Initiative Act, NQIA reauthorization), EU (Quantum Flagship, €1B), China (estimated $15B+ in national quantum investment), and UK/Japan/Australia are all racing. This is a structural tailwind — government-funded quantum progress will happen even if private markets cool.

Divergence from Historical Patterns — The Critical Caveat

Quantum computing may diverge from historical analogs in important ways that affect timeline estimates:

  1. The scaling challenge is fundamentally different. Semiconductors scaled because smaller transistors were better — faster, cheaper, lower power. Quantum computing does not necessarily scale with miniaturization. Scaling means maintaining coherence across an exponentially growing Hilbert space — a fundamentally harder problem. There is no quantum equivalent of Moore's Law, and the presence of an uncertainty relation means you cannot simultaneously measure and preserve quantum state. This is a physics constraint, not an engineering one.

  2. Quantum cannot automate its own design (yet). Classical computing accelerated its own development — better computers designed better chips (EDA tools, simulation). Quantum computers may eventually accelerate quantum simulation, but NISQ-era devices are too noisy to design larger quantum devices. This self-reinforcing loop is absent.

  3. Classical alternatives are actively improving. The historical analogs (transistors replacing vacuum tubes, digital replacing analog) had a clear, monotonic advantage. Quantum computing competes with a classical ecosystem that is ~$600B/year in R&D investment and accelerating due to AI. Classical computing may absorb some of quantum's potential TAM before quantum becomes practical.

  4. Quantum may not need to win universally. A narrower but still valuable path exists: quantum computers as specialized accelerators for specific simulation and optimization problems, integrated into classical HPC workflows. This is the "FPGA" or "GPU" model, not the "general-purpose processor" model. The timeline to this outcome may be shorter (2028–2032) even if general-purpose fault-tolerant quantum computing takes longer.

What This Suggests for Quantum

Infrastructure layer likely captures first-mover value: - Hardware manufacturers (IonQ, Quantinuum, Google Quantum AI) will be the analog of early Intel or Illumina — companies that control the scaling roadmap and sell access to the physical layer. - Cloud providers (AWS Braket, Azure Quantum, Google) already capture distribution value, similar to infrastructure plays in AI (AWS SageMaker) and internet hosting. - The "fab" model (Rigetti's chip foundry, IonQ's SkyWater acquisition) may be strategically analogous to owning semiconductor fabrication in the 1960s — a high-cost, high-barrier position with long-term rent capture.

Platform / software is the most uncertain layer: - Unlike classical computing (where Microsoft captured enormous OS value), quantum may not have a dominant software platform, or the software value may accrue to the hardware vendors (like NVIDIA's CUDA, not a standalone OS). - IBM's Qiskit is the closest to a dominant SDK, but it is open-source and tied to IBM hardware. No quantum company has captured meaningful software-only value, analogous to Microsoft or Oracle. - The "quantum compiler" layer — mapping abstract algorithms to specific hardware topologies — could be valuable, but only after architectural consolidation.

Applications layer is premature: - Investing in quantum application companies today is like investing in internet content companies in 1992 — the platform isn't ready, the user base doesn't exist, and the business models are speculative. - The exception: vertical-specific quantum consulting and algorithm development (QC Ware, Zapata Computing, 1QBit) may find profitable niches before full fault-tolerance, serving early adopters in pharma, defense, and finance who are prototyping for the fault-tolerant era.

This is a high-conviction bet on a specific technology (trapped ions) and business model (full-stack + cloud + M&A).
  • D-Wave trades at ~167x trailing EV/S on $24.6M revenue ($884.5M cash). The market is pricing annealing as a real (but uncertain) commercial market, plus some probability that the QCI gate-model bet works.
  • Rigetti trades at ~423x trailing P/S on $7.1M declining revenue ($590M cash). This is the highest-risk/reward — a call option on a superconducting turnaround.

The valuation distribution mirrors early AI investing: a few companies of high confidence (IonQ = NVIDIA?), a middle with a differentiated bet (D-Wave = a specialized AI chip company?), and a high-risk contender with a plausible-but-unproven technology (Rigetti = the startup that might disappear or break out).

Where Value May Accrue in Quantum

Based on the historical patterns:

  1. Hardware is the safest bet — the companies that build the first fault-tolerant systems with practical quantum advantage will capture the bulk of early value, because the technology is the constraint. This is analogous to Intel in 1971 (just before the 4004) or Illumina in 2005 (just before next-gen sequencing took off).

  2. Error correction IP will be disproportionately valuable — analogous to planar process patents in semiconductors or the CRISPR IP portfolio. Companies that own the best error correction codes, the lowest-overhead architectures, and the implementable protocols will collect licensing or competitive advantage.

  3. Vertical integration (hardware + cloud + applications) may win in the near term — IonQ's model (triple-cloud distribution, on-prem systems, government contracts, pending SkyWater fab) mirrors IBM's full-stack approach in the 1960s. In a pre-standardized market, owning the full stack lets you capture value at every layer.

  4. Standardized modular components will eventually emerge — but not until architectural consolidation. The analog is semiconductor foundries (TSMC) emerging only after the industry standardized on CMOS (1980s–90s). Quantum foundry services (Rigetti's strategy) may be early by 10+ years.

  5. The "killer app" company may not exist yet — similar to how Google (founded 1998) didn't exist in the internet's pre-1993 phase. The company that figures out the first genuinely useful quantum application may be founded between now and 2030.

Conclusion

Quantum computing today most closely resembles artificial intelligence in the 1998–2004 period — the years between Deep Blue's victory over Kasparov and the algorithmic/engineering preconditions for the deep learning revolution.

The science is real. The engineering path is understood in principle but unproven at scale. Real commercial revenue exists but is measured in the hundreds of millions, not billions. No killer application has emerged. Multiple architectures compete without a dominant winner. The classical alternatives are not standing still.

Do not assume linear progress. Quantum computing could diverge from historical patterns in either direction. The best case (2030 fault-tolerant quantum advantage) requires compression of engineering timelines that have historically taken 15–25 years in other technologies. The worst case (2040+ or never) reflects the genuine possibility that quantum scaling faces physics-level constraints that no amount of engineering can overcome — a risk that semiconductors, the internet, and sequencing did not face.

The most probable base case: modern quantum computers reach practical utility for specific, high-value problems in materials science, chemistry, and optimization between 2031 and 2035, following a trajectory closer to the Human Genome Project (sustained, predictable, government-funded long-term progress) than to the internet (explosive, viral, consumer-driven). At that point, the first $1B+ quantum computing company will have established itself, and the "pre-breakout" investors will have been rewarded — but those who bought extreme valuations (400x+ trailing sales) after the 2024–2026 hype cycle may need patience.

The most honest single sentence to describe quantum computing's position today: We know it works. We know what it would take to make it commercially transformative. We do not know how long that will take, whether classical alternatives will absorb part of its TAM before it arrives, or which architecture will win. That is exactly where AI was in 2001, where the internet was in 1992, and where semiconductors were in 1955. It is a good place to be a skeptic, an even better place to be an investor with a 15-year horizon, and a terrible place to be an investor with a 2-year horizon.
